Glossary of budget and Town terms

99 words used in Riverhead’s budgets, audits and Town Board meetings, each in a sentence or two of plain English.

Independent, unofficial project·Data last refreshed November 11, 2026·Recently refreshed·See data update details →

In plain English

Town finance has its own vocabulary. This page explains each word the site uses, in the order you would look it up. For how the ideas fit together, such as why the Town keeps separate funds or why the audit and the budget can show different numbers, read How the Town’s books work.
  • Search: type part of a word. It looks in the definitions too, so “savings” finds fund balance and reserves.
  • Topics: pick one of the 8 topics to see only those terms.
  • Where it matters: most terms link to the page on this site where the figure appears.
ABCDEFGHILMNOPRSTUV

99 terms.

A

Abstain
To formally decline to vote yes or no — often because a member has a conflict of interest on that item.Town Board
Account / Line item
The smallest level of budget detail — a single spending line, such as "Police – Overtime." Each has an account code that says its fund, purpose and type.SpendingWhere it matters: Budget by fund
Adopted Budget
The budget the Town Board adopts by resolution, no later than November 20 (Town Law §109). It is the only version that appropriates money — the legal authority to spend. The Town sometimes titles it the Final Budget.Budget and processWhere it matters: How the budget is adopted
Annual Financial Report (AFR)
The report each town files with the State Comptroller every year on its actual revenue, spending and balances. The Comptroller’s fiscal stress scores are built from it.Accounting and reportsWhere it matters: Annual financial report
Appropriated fund balance (savings used)
Savings from earlier years that a fund counts as income to balance this year’s budget, so less has to come from taxes or charges. It pays toward the whole budget, not one item. Using some is common; a fund that needs a large share of it every year is planning to spend more than it takes in.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: How savings were used
Appropriations
The money the Town plans to spend, approved line by line. An appropriation is the legal permission to spend up to that amount for that purpose.Budget and processWhere it matters: Budget by fund
Assessed value
The value the Town Assessor puts on a property for taxes. In Riverhead it is a small fraction of what the property would sell for, so it looks low; the tax rate is set to match.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Your tax bill
Assigned fund balance
Money earmarked for an intended use without a formal Board commitment, such as next year’s budget or purchases already ordered.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: How the Town’s books work
Audited financial statements
The Town’s yearly financial statements, examined by an independent accounting firm. The auditor’s opinion says whether they fairly present the Town’s finances.Accounting and reportsWhere it matters: Annual financial report
Authorized but unissued
Borrowing the Board has approved but the Town has not yet done. It is not yet debt, but it shows what is coming.Borrowing and debtWhere it matters: Projects and borrowing

B

BAN (Bond Anticipation Note)
Short-term borrowing the Town later pays back or replaces with a long-term bond.Borrowing and debtWhere it matters: Projects and borrowing
Bond
Long-term borrowing, repaid with interest over years. Town bonds are usually serial bonds, with part of the principal due each year.Borrowing and debtWhere it matters: Projects and borrowing
Bond resolution
The Board’s permission to borrow for a stated purpose up to a stated amount. It needs a two-thirds vote. Borrowing happens only later, when bonds or notes are sold.Borrowing and debtWhere it matters: The new Justice Court
Budget note
Short-term borrowing to pay for spending the budget did not include. State law ordinarily caps it at 5 percent of the budget, so heavy use is a warning sign.Borrowing and debt
Budget officer
The official who assembles the department requests into the Tentative Budget. In Riverhead, as in most towns, it is the Supervisor.Budget and process
Budget public hearing
The meeting, required by Town Law §108, where residents can speak on the Preliminary Budget before it is adopted. The notice must print the proposed salaries of the Board members, the Town Clerk and the Highway Superintendent.Budget and processWhere it matters: How the budget is adopted
Budget Supplement
The account-by-account detail published alongside the Tentative Budget. For every line it shows the actual from two years back, last year’s adopted amount and spending to June 30, what the department requested, and what the Tentative recommends.Budget and processWhere it matters: Budget by fund
Budget transfer / adjustment
A Board resolution that moves money between budget lines after the budget is adopted, or adds money from savings or a grant. It changes the plan, not the total taxes already levied.Budget and processWhere it matters: Budget changes the Board approved
Budget-to-actual variance
The difference between what a line was budgeted (its appropriation) and what was actually spent. Spending below the budget leaves money that lapses into fund balance; spending above it needs a budget transfer from another line.SpendingWhere it matters: Budget vs. actual spending
Budgetary basis
Counting the way the budget does: spending counts when money is committed, including encumbrances. It answers “did we stay within what was approved?”Accounting and reportsWhere it matters: How the Town’s books work

C

Capital project
Building or buying something that lasts for years, such as a building, road or major vehicle, usually paid for over time with borrowing. Its money does not lapse at year end.SpendingWhere it matters: Projects and borrowing
Committed fund balance
Money the Town Board has set aside by its own formal action. Only another formal Board action can free it.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: How the Town’s books work
Compensated absences
The accounting name for unused sick, vacation, and personal time that employees have earned and the Town will eventually have to pay for. It appears as a liability on the Town’s audited books because it is money owed, even though nobody has cashed it out yet.Pay and benefits
Constitutional debt limit
The cap the State Constitution puts on a town’s debt: 7 percent of the average full value of its taxable property over five years.Borrowing and debtWhere it matters: Projects and borrowing
Contingency appropriation
A cushion inside the budget for unexpected costs, capped at 10 percent of certain appropriations (Town Law §107). Nothing is paid from it directly: the Board first transfers money from it to a real spending line.Budget and process
Contractual
Day-to-day operating costs paid to outside vendors — supplies, utilities, repairs, and professional services.Spending
Controllable cost
A cost the Town Board and departments can change by their own decisions in the year, such as how much fuel the fleet uses. Prices set by others, State-mandated pension and insurance costs, and amounts fixed by union contracts are not controllable in the short term.SpendingWhere it matters: Budget vs. actual spending
Credit rating
A rating agency’s grade on how likely the Town is to repay its debt. A higher rating usually means lower interest costs.Borrowing and debtWhere it matters: Credit rating

D

Debt Service
Payments on money the Town has borrowed — similar to making mortgage payments.Borrowing and debtWhere it matters: Projects and borrowing
Department
A unit of town government within a fund — for example Police or Town Clerk inside the General Fund.Spending

E

Early Retirement Buyout / Incentive
A one-time payment the Town offers to encourage eligible longtime employees to retire now, so it can hold positions open, combine roles, or refill them at lower cost.Pay and benefitsWhere it matters: 2026 retirement buyout
Employee Benefits
Health insurance, retirement, and other non-salary costs of employing people.SpendingWhere it matters: Fringe benefits
Encumbrance
Money set aside within an appropriation for an order already placed but not yet paid. It keeps the same money from being spent twice.Spending
Equipment & Capital Outlay
Bigger one-time purchases such as vehicles, machinery, and equipment.Spending
ERS
The New York State & Local Employees’ Retirement System — the state pension system most non-police town workers belong to.Pay and benefits
Estimated Revenues
Money the Town expects to take in from things other than property taxes — fees, permits, state aid, interest, and so on.Budget and processWhere it matters: Revenue
Executive session
A closed part of a meeting, allowed by the Open Meetings Law only for listed topics such as litigation or personnel. It must be called in public, by a majority vote that names the topic.Town BoardWhere it matters: Open Meetings Law
Exemption
Part of a property’s assessed value that is not taxed, such as a veterans’, senior citizens’ or disability exemption. Some exemptions lower only certain taxes.Taxes and revenue
Expenditure
Money a government fund spends or owes during the year, counted under the modified accrual basis. Governments record expenditures, not the business “costs” of making a product, and set them against the appropriation that allowed them.SpendingWhere it matters: How the Town’s books work

F

Fiscal impact statement
The form attached to each Riverhead Town Board resolution saying whether it costs money, how much, and which account pays. It is the Town’s own summary, so this site checks it against the resolution’s text.Budget and processWhere it matters: Cost of Board decisions
Fiscal stress score
The State Comptroller’s yearly rating of each local government’s financial condition, from its Annual Financial Report: no designation, susceptible, moderate or significant stress.Accounting and reportsWhere it matters: Financial health
Fiscal year
The twelve months a budget covers. For New York towns it is the calendar year, January 1 to December 31.Budget and process
Fixed cost
A cost that stays about the same however much service the Town provides, such as an allowance set per employee by a union contract. A budgeting term for what drives a cost, not a category in the Town’s books.SpendingWhere it matters: Recurring costs by category
Franchise fee
What a cable company pays the Town for using Town roads and rights-of-way, set as a share of its cable revenue. Federal law caps it at 5 percent.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Optimum cable franchise
Full accrual
How the Town-wide statements count, as a business would: revenue when earned and costs when incurred, with buildings, roads, debt, pensions and retiree health care all included.Accounting and reportsWhere it matters: How the Town’s books work
Function (account code)
The part of an account number that says which service the money is for: 3120 is Police, 5142 is snow removal, 1620 is Town Hall. The State Comptroller’s chart of accounts sets the codes for every town.SpendingWhere it matters: Recurring costs by category
Fund
A separate pot of money set aside for a specific purpose — like Highway, Water, or Sewer. Each fund has its own budget and its own savings, and money cannot move between funds without Board action.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: Budget by fund
Fund Balance
A fund’s savings: what is left after years of revenue minus spending. Only part of it is free to spend; accounting rules sort it into five tiers.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: Savings and reserves
Fund balance contribution
A budget line that sets money aside in a fund’s savings. It is listed with the spending, but the money is meant to stay in the fund.Funds and savings

G

GAAP
Generally Accepted Accounting Principles: the standard rules for financial statements. For state and local governments they are set by GASB.Accounting and reportsWhere it matters: How the Town’s books work
GASB
The Governmental Accounting Standards Board, which writes the accounting rules for state and local governments, such as Statement No. 54 on fund balance.Accounting and reports
General Fund
The main pot of money that pays for most town-wide services, like police, the town clerk, and general administration.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: General Fund
Gross Pay
The total an employee was paid in a year before deductions — including base pay, overtime, and extras.Pay and benefitsWhere it matters: Employee pay and overtime

H

Health insurance buy-back
A payment to an employee who declines the Town’s health coverage — usually because they are covered by a spouse’s plan. The Town pays them part of what the premium would have cost. Despite the similar name, this has nothing to do with sick or vacation time.Pay and benefits
Home rule request
A local government’s formal request that the State Legislature pass a bill that affects only it. The State Constitution requires one for most such bills.Town BoardWhere it matters: The former armory and the YMCA

I

Interfund / Transfers
Money moved from one of the Town’s funds to another. A transfer is permanent; a loan between funds must be repaid by the end of the year.Funds and savings

L

Lapse
What happens to an operating appropriation that is neither spent nor encumbered by year end: the permission to spend it ends, and the money stays in fund balance.Spending
Local law
A change to the Town Code, such as zoning or a tax cap override. It needs a public hearing before the Board adopts it.Town Board

M

Market (full) value
What a property would likely sell for. Dividing assessed value by the residential assessment ratio gives an estimate of it.Taxes and revenue
Modified accrual
How the audited fund statements count: revenue when it is measurable and available to pay this year’s bills, and spending when the bill comes due. Long-term debt is left out.Accounting and reportsWhere it matters: How the Town’s books work
Mover / Seconder
The member who formally proposes a resolution ("moves" it) and the member who supports bringing it to a vote ("seconds" it).Town Board

N

Net pension liability
The Town’s share of the gap between what the State pension systems owe and what they hold, as the audit reports it.Accounting and reports
Net position
In the Town-wide statements, everything the Town owns minus everything it owes. Long-term obligations such as pensions and retiree health care can make it negative.Accounting and reports
Nonspendable fund balance
The part of fund balance that is not cash at all, such as prepaid bills or inventory. It cannot be spent.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: How the Town’s books work

O

Object of expenditure (object code)
The part of an account number that says what was bought: 111 is overtime, 426 vehicle fuel, 472 electricity. Grouping lines by object code shows one kind of cost across every department.SpendingWhere it matters: Recurring costs by category
One-time money
Money that comes once, such as savings, an asset sale or a one-off grant. Using it for costs that recur every year leaves the same gap next year.Accounting and reports
OPEB
Other post-employment benefits: mainly health insurance the Town has promised retirees. The audit reports the cost of those promises as a liability.Accounting and reports
Overtime
Extra pay for hours an employee works beyond their normal schedule.Pay and benefitsWhere it matters: Employee pay and overtime

P

Period of probable usefulness
The useful life the Local Finance Law assigns to what is being financed. Bonds for it cannot run longer than that.Borrowing and debt
Permissive referendum
A Board action that voters can force to a public vote by filing a petition within 30 days. If no valid petition is filed, the action takes effect. Many bond resolutions are subject to one.Town Board
Personal Services
Salaries and wages paid to Town employees.Spending
PFRS
The New York State Police and Fire Retirement System — the state pension system for police officers and firefighters.Pay and benefits
PILOT
A payment in lieu of taxes: what some businesses pay instead of property tax under an agreement with the Town or its Industrial Development Agency.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Revenue
Preliminary Budget
The Tentative Budget plus any changes the Town Board makes to it (Town Law §106(4)). The required public hearing is held on this version, by the Thursday after the general election (§108). If the Board fails to adopt a budget by November 20, the Preliminary becomes the budget (§109(3)).Budget and processWhere it matters: Budget amendments
Property tax cap
The State limit on how much a town’s levy may grow each year: generally 2 percent or the rate of inflation, whichever is lower, with adjustments (General Municipal Law §3-c).Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Property-tax cap
Public hearing
A session where anyone may speak on a proposal before the Board acts, required for local laws, the budget and some other actions. Written comments are often accepted for a time afterward.Town Board

R

Reconciled
Our totals add up exactly to the Town’s official published numbers, so you can trust the detail behind them.Accounting and reports
Regular / Base Pay
An employee’s normal salary or wages, not counting overtime or extras.Pay and benefits
Reserve fund
Savings set aside under a specific State law for a stated purpose, such as capital projects, repairs or retirement costs. Spending from it follows that law’s rules.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: Savings and reserves
Reserve Use
Spending savings instead of recurring income. It can balance a single budget but is risky if repeated year after year.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: How savings were used
Residential assessment ratio
The share of market value at which homes are assessed, published by the State for each assessment roll. It converts market value to assessed value and back.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Your tax bill
Resolution
A formal item the Town Board votes on — approving a contract, setting a fee, authorizing spending, or making an appointment.Town BoardWhere it matters: Board votes
Restricted fund balance
Money that outside rules, such as a law, grant or bond, say can only be used for a stated purpose.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: How the Town’s books work

S

Semi-variable cost
A cost with a fixed part and a part that varies, such as electricity: a monthly service charge plus the power actually used.SpendingWhere it matters: Recurring costs by category
Severance
A payment made because employment is ending, set by contract or agreement. Unlike a leave buy-back it is not time the employee banked and earned — which is why the two should not be read as the same thing even though they land in the same payroll column.Pay and benefits
Sick / vacation buy-back
Employees earn sick and vacation days each year, and unused days often carry over. A buy-back is the Town paying cash for some of those banked days instead of letting the person take the time off — either periodically during a career, or as one payment when they leave. The days were already earned, so this is settling a debt, not a bonus.Pay and benefits
Special district
An area within the Town that pays for a service only it receives, such as water, sewer, street lighting or ambulance. Only properties in the district pay its tax or charges.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Budget by fund
STAR
The State’s School Tax Relief program. It lowers school taxes only, so it does not change the Town portion of a bill.Taxes and revenue
State aid
Money the State sends the Town, either for general purposes or tied to a program such as highways or a grant.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Grants
Structural balance
When recurring revenue pays for recurring costs, without leaning on savings or one-time money. A budget can be balanced on paper and still not be structurally balanced.Accounting and reportsWhere it matters: Budget best practices (GFOA)

T

TAN (Tax Anticipation Note)
Short-term borrowing to pay bills until taxes come in, repaid from those taxes.Borrowing and debt
Tax cap carryover
If the Town levies less than its cap allows, it may carry some of the unused room into the next year, up to 1.5 percent of the levy limit.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Property-tax cap
Tax cap override
A local law that lets the Town levy more than the cap allows. It must be adopted before the budget, by at least 60 percent of the Board.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Property-tax cap
Tax Levy
The total amount the Town collects from property taxes to cover whatever the other revenues do not pay for.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Where your Town levy goes
Tax rate
The tax charged per $1,000 of assessed value. Each fund or district with a levy has its own rate; your bill adds them up.Taxes and revenueWhere it matters: Your tax bill
Tentative Budget
The budget officer’s recommended budget, filed with the Town Clerk by September 30 and presented to the Town Board by October 5 (Town Law §106). It is a proposal and authorizes no spending. It usually comes with a Budget Supplement showing what each department asked for.Budget and processWhere it matters: The 2027 budget

U

Unassigned fund balance
The part of the General Fund’s savings with no label on it: the real cushion for emergencies and shortfalls.Funds and savingsWhere it matters: Savings and reserves
Union / Bargaining Group
The labor group an employee belongs to — for example PBA for police officers or CSEA for many town workers. Its contract with the Town sets pay and benefits for its members.Pay and benefits

V

Variable cost
A cost that rises and falls with how much service is provided, such as vehicle fuel (miles driven times the price per gallon) or overtime after storms. A budgeting term, not a category in the Town’s books.SpendingWhere it matters: Recurring costs by category

These definitions are a plain-English aid written for this site, not legal definitions. Where a term comes from State law or an accounting standard, the page it links to names the source. Underlined words with a small question mark elsewhere on the site open the same definitions.
