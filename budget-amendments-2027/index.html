The amendments to the 2027 budget

The Town Board adopted six amendments on October 20, with no votes against. They would raise $643,858 less in town-wide property taxes than the Supervisor’s Tentative. What each one changes, line by line, and what it means for savings and a tax bill.

Independent project·Updated October 24, 2026·Current·Check data freshness →

In plain English

The Board adopted all six on October 20, 2026, with no votes against, and they become part of the Preliminary Budget the Board will hold a hearing on. Once the budget is adopted, the six would raise $62,238,344 in town-wide property taxes, $643,858 less than the Tentative, so the levy would rise 1.73% over 2026 instead of 2.79%. Of the cut, $444,570 comes from higher estimates of what the Town will collect in fees, fines and interest, and $201,237 from taking equipment out of the budget, to be bought from savings by separate votes during 2027. One amendment adds $1,949. Three of the six add pay or insurance costs. RiverheadLOCAL reports that Council Member Ken Rothwell proposed them.
  • Preliminary budget: the Supervisor’s Tentative as the Board changes it. The Board holds a public hearing on it, then adopts a budget by November 20.
  • Levy: the total the Town raises from property taxes. The town-wide levy pays for the General, Highway and Street Lighting funds.
  • Savings: “fund balance”: money left from earlier years. A budget can count some of it as income, called appropriated fund balance.

At a glance

The levy would still grow, by $1,060,052 over 2026 instead of the Tentative’s $1,703,910. Spending across all funds would fall by $104,659: the six take $232,000 out of equipment and snow-removal lines and add $127,341 of pay and health insurance.

The Supervisor’s letter says “There is joy in presenting this budget as all taxing districts are budgeted within the tax cap limit of 2.79%.” With the six, the town-wide levy would rise less than that. The letter gives the limit only as a percentage, and no Town document prints its dollar amount (2027 Tentative).

The six amendments

Each resolution lists the accounts it changes in two columns, “from” and “to”. Money comes from a smaller appropriation or a higher revenue estimate, and goes to a larger appropriation or a smaller revenue: less property tax, or less savings used. Each one balances within each fund. The numbers are the Town’s; RiverheadLOCAL describes the same six in a different order.

Amendment #1: take three equipment purchases out of the General Fund budget

Its reason, as printed: “upon review of the 2027 Tentative Budget, the Town Board wishes to make additional reductions to the property tax levy for 2027”, and “several one-time expenditures were identified in the budget which can be removed from the budget and instead funded with General Fund balance via the submission of separate Town Board resolutions in 2027”.

It lowers the General Fund levy by $158,300. It would leave $4,576 for buildings and grounds equipment and $11,530 for fire marshal equipment. Refuse vehicles, a line new in 2027, would get nothing. Adopted on October 20, 2026 as Resolution 2026-935: moved by Councilman Robert Kern, seconded by Councilwoman Denise Merrifield; ayes, Kenneth Rothwell, Robert Kern, Denise Merrifield and Joann Waski; nays, none (packet with results, p. 34).

Amendment #2: a merit increase in the Highway Department’s office, paid for by the General Fund levy

Its reason, as printed: “the Highway Superintendent requested an adjustment to the 2027 Preliminary Budget for an additional merit increase for an administrative employee in the General Fund”.

It raises the General Fund levy by $1,949. Adopted on October 20, 2026 as Resolution 2026-936: moved by Councilwoman Denise Merrifield, seconded by Councilwoman Joann Waski; ayes, Kenneth Rothwell, Robert Kern, Denise Merrifield and Joann Waski; nays, none (packet with results, p. 36).

Amendment #3: raise three General Fund revenue estimates

Its reason, as printed: “upon review of the 2027 Tentative Budget, the Town Board wishes to make additional reductions to the property tax levy and appropriated fund balance for 2027”.

It lowers the General Fund levy by $395,941 and counts $70,000 less of the General Fund’s savings as income. The three estimates rise by $465,941 in all (see the higher revenue estimates). Adopted on October 20, 2026 as Resolution 2026-937: moved by Councilwoman Joann Waski, seconded by Councilman Kenneth Rothwell; ayes, Kenneth Rothwell, Robert Kern, Denise Merrifield and Joann Waski; nays, none (packet with results, p. 38).

Amendment #4: a promotion and a merit increase in the Highway Fund, paid for from its snow budget

Its reason, as printed: “the Highway Superintendent requested an adjustment to the 2027 Preliminary Budget for an additional promotion for one employee and an additional merit increase for one employee in the Highway Fund”.

It leaves every levy where the Tentative put it. The Tentative had raised the snow budget to $400,000, from $225,000 in 2026; this takes $5,392 of it. Adopted on October 20, 2026 as Resolution 2026-938: moved by Councilman Kenneth Rothwell, seconded by Councilman Robert Kern; ayes, Kenneth Rothwell, Robert Kern, Denise Merrifield and Joann Waski; nays, none (packet with results, p. 40).

Amendment #5: take street lighting equipment out and raise the district’s interest estimate

Its reason, as printed: “upon review of the 2027 Tentative Budget, the Town Board wishes to make additional reductions to the property tax levy for 2027”, and “one-time expenditures for Street Lighting equipment can be removed from the budget and instead funded with Street Lighting fund balance via the submission of separate Town Board resolutions in 2027”.

It lowers the Street Lighting District levy by $91,566. Street lighting equipment would get nothing; the Tentative proposed $42,937. Adopted on October 20, 2026 as Resolution 2026-939: moved by Councilman Robert Kern, seconded by Councilwoman Denise Merrifield; ayes, Jerry Halpin, Kenneth Rothwell, Robert Kern, Denise Merrifield and Joann Waski; nays, none (packet with results, p. 42).

Amendment #6: money for a possible change in CSEA dental plans

Its reason, as printed: “the Town Board wishes to provide funding for a possible change in dental insurance plans for the CSEA in the 2027 Preliminary Budget”.

It leaves every levy where the Tentative put it. The $120,000 goes to health insurance lines in seven funds, paid for by $74,059 more in expected building inspection fees, $20,570 more in expected interest in five districts and $25,371 less for Highway equipment. Adopted on October 20, 2026 as Resolution 2026-940: moved by Councilwoman Denise Merrifield, seconded by Councilwoman Joann Waski; ayes, Kenneth Rothwell, Robert Kern, Denise Merrifield and Joann Waski; nays, none (packet with results, p. 45).

By fund

The higher revenue estimates

Amendments #3, #5 and #6 raise eight estimates of what the Town will collect, by $609,199 in all. A higher estimate is not cash in hand. If 2027 collections fall short of it, the difference comes out of the fund’s savings at the end of the year, or out of spending.

Interest

The General Fund’s budgets have long estimated far less interest than it earned. Every budget from 2019 to 2026 estimated $50,000 or less. The Town collected $1,340,200 in 2023, $1,935,791 in 2024 and $1,491,222 in 2025, and $636,132 in the first half of 2026. The Tentative estimates $750,000 for 2027; Amendment #3 would make it $1,000,000.

RiverheadLOCAL reports Rothwell’s figure: “He said the town earned $4.8 million in investment returns on its fund balance.” The General Fund’s collections for 2023 to 2025 add to $4,767,213, which rounds to that. The article does not say what period he meant. Interest depends on rates and on how much cash the Town holds, so it can fall even when the Town does nothing differently.

Building inspection fees

Amendments #3 and #6 together raise this estimate by $240,000, to $1,440,000. The Town collected $1,486,440 in 2024 and $1,686,044 in 2025. In the first half of 2026 it collected $529,984, 44% of that year’s $1,200,000 estimate. Fees follow construction, which can slow.

Fines and forfeited bail

Amendment #3 raises this estimate by $50,000, to $700,000. The Town collected $538,166 in 2024 and $865,515 in 2025, and $241,146 in the first half of 2026.

The districts’ interest

The Tentative estimates $500 or less of interest for each of the water, sewer, scavenger waste and street lighting districts. The amendments raise those estimates by $69,199 in all, to amounts still below what each district collected in 2025.

What happens to savings

Amendment #3 would count $70,000 less of the General Fund’s savings as income in 2027: $880,000 instead of $950,000.

Amendments #1 and #5 take $201,237 of equipment out of the budget: $53,300 of buildings and grounds equipment, $50,000 of fire marshal equipment, $55,000 of refuse vehicles and $42,937 of street lighting equipment. Both say the purchases would instead be “funded with … fund balance via the submission of separate Town Board resolutions in 2027.” The purchases would still be made, paid from savings by votes of their own during the year rather than through the levy. In RiverheadLOCAL’s words, “Paying for the equipment later from fund balance would change how the purchases are financed rather than eliminate their cost.”

Newsday describes it differently: “The first amendment uses surplus funds to cover $158,300 in equipment rather than the general fund.” The equipment is still General Fund equipment; what changes is that the levy no longer pays for it and savings would, by separate votes. Of Amendment #6 it says, “It also relies on a $25,371 transfer from the highway equipment fund.” There is no separate equipment fund: Amendment #6 cuts the Highway Fund’s own equipment line by $25,371 and moves it to that fund’s health insurance. On the equipment, it reports: “Halpin pushed back and said the town buys those items annually.” If so, the same purchases would come back in later budgets.

If the Board later approves all of them, the General Fund would use $88,300 more of its savings in 2027 than the Tentative planned ($158,300 of equipment against $70,000 less in the budget), and the Street Lighting District $42,937 more. For scale, the General Fund’s unassigned fund balance was $28,829,513 at the end of 2025, by the audit.

Paying for equipment from savings uses one-time money for a one-time cost, the rule of thumb in this site’s guide to one-time money and recurring costs. Rothwell, as RiverheadLOCAL reports him: “we never want to use fund balance for ongoing costs.”

Three amendments add costs that carry into later years once granted: merit increases and a promotion (#2 and #4), and money for a possible change in dental plans (#6), $127,341 in all. They are paid for with $1,949 of levy, $5,392 from the snow budget, $25,371 from Highway equipment and $94,629 of higher revenue estimates. An equipment cut saves money once; a raise is in every budget after.

Of the $232,000 the six take out of spending, $201,237 is equipment to be bought later. The other $30,763 ($25,371 of Highway equipment and $5,392 of the snow budget) is moved to pay and insurance, and the resolutions do not say whether those lines would be made up.

What it means for a tax bill

The Supervisor’s letter gives the cost of the General Fund’s increase for “property valued at $800,000”. At the 2025–26 residential assessment ratio of 7.44%, that home is assessed at about $59,520. Before any exemption, its town-wide tax would be about $4,358 under the Tentative and $4,314 with the six, about $44 a year less. In 2026 the same assessment paid $4,262. To use your own assessed value, try the tax bill estimator.

Newsday reports the Financial Administrator’s own figures for the same home: “the projected tax increase would be $55 per year for a home assessed at $800,000, according to town financial administrator Jeanette DiPaola”, and “Halpin’s initial proposal would carry a roughly $101 increase for a home with the same market value.” Against 2026, this site’s estimate is about $53 with the six and $97 under the Tentative. The small difference likely comes from rounding or the assessment ratio used; hers are the Town’s figures.

Where this stands

The Board adopted all six on October 20, 2026, as Resolutions 2026-935 to 2026-940, with no votes against any of them. The Town reposted the meeting’s agenda packet with each result on October 21, 2026, and the resolutions it shows match the draft this page was built from, account for account and word for word.

On five of the six the packet lists the four council members as the ayes and no one as a nay. Supervisor Halpin is listed as an aye only on Amendment #5, the street lighting amendment. He voted on the other resolutions before and after them that night. The packet’s vote record has lines only for ayes and nays, so it does not show how he was recorded on the other five. RiverheadLOCAL reports: “Five resolutions passed 4–1, with Halpin abstaining. The street lighting amendment passed unanimously.” The minutes, which are not out yet, will be the Town’s record.

The article gives each side’s reasons. Halpin: “I feel like this is rooted in political motivation,” and “This type of budgeting can come back to bite us in years ahead.” On street lighting he said, “I think this is a risk, but I agree. We’ve been overtaxing street lighting for years, so yes.” Rothwell, on the interest estimate: “It’s an adjustment on interest earnings so that we’re not overtaxing.” Councilwoman Merrifield: “I’m very happy that we are able to come in lower than the supervisor’s.”

The six amend the Preliminary Budget. They do not adopt a budget, and the Board can still change it after the public hearing. What comes next:

  • October 29, 2026: the Preliminary Budget, with the six amendments in it, is available on the Town’s website and in the Town Clerk’s office (Notice with Resolution 2026-923)
  • November 5, 2026, 2:05 p.m.: public hearing on the 2027 budget, including elected officials’ salaries, at Town Hall (Notice with Resolution 2026-923)
  • November 17, 2026: the only Board meeting on the Town’s calendar between the hearing and the deadline; the site’s editor reports the resolution to adopt the budget will be submitted then (Town meeting calendar)
  • November 20, 2026: deadline to adopt; if the Board does not, the Preliminary becomes the budget (Town Law §109)

RiverheadLOCAL writes “A public hearing is scheduled for Nov. 4.” The Town’s published notice, adopted with Resolution 2026-923, sets the hearing for November 5 at 2:05 p.m., and the Town’s meeting calendar lists November 5. Newsday writes “A public hearing on the 2027 budget will be held at Town Hall at 2 p.m. on Nov. 5.” This page follows the Town’s notice.

Newsday’s report adds: “Rothwell’s proposal would raise about $640,000 less in tax revenue than Halpin's.” The six lower the town-wide levy by $643,858, which matches. Rothwell told it, “Our fund balance is plenty, and we don't need to keep adding to it.” Of last year’s increase he said, “We did plan for the worst, which unfortunately meant raising taxes last year.”

It also reports: “Halpin cited the town’s 2022 budget, which did not raise the tax levy and instead relied on $2 million in surplus funding to balance the budget.” The 2022 Adopted Budget bears out the first part: the town-wide levy fell from $49,664,100 to $48,021,203, partly because some of it was moved to payments in lieu of taxes. It does not show $2,000,000 of savings used in the town-wide funds: the General, Highway and Street Lighting funds appropriated $0, and the $4,437,800 the budget did use was in the Community Preservation, sewer and water funds. The General Fund budget that planned a $2,000,000 draw was 2024’s. Savings the Board voted to use during 2022, after the budget was adopted, would not appear in it.

RiverheadLOCAL reports that Rothwell said he developed the amendments with the town’s financial administrator and the other council members. “He said all the other council members support them.” Rothwell is challenging Supervisor Halpin in the November 3 election. The article says “Halpin did not respond to a request for comment as of presstime.”

Rothwell said “we did not decrease or remove any services”. The resolutions cut no position or program line. They do take money from Highway equipment and the snow budget, as above. For how earlier Boards changed a Supervisor’s budget, especially in election years, see how budgets get adopted.

How it bears on the supervisor’s race

This is a reading of how each side could use the amendments, weighed evenly, as on this site’s candidate cost-benefit page. It is not a prediction, and the site supports no candidate. Supervisor Halpin, on the Democratic line and not enrolled in a party, faces a Board of four Republican council members, one of whom, Rothwell, is running against him.

For Rothwell, the challenger

  • Could help. It turns his campaign’s stated top issue, lowering the cost of taxes, into six resolutions the Board adopted two weeks before the election, each with all four council members voting yes. It sets a 1.73% increase beside the incumbent’s 2.79%, and by his account “we did not decrease or remove any services”.
  • Could hurt. $444,570 of the $643,858 cut rests on higher revenue estimates and $201,237 on buying equipment from savings later, which critics can call a change of financing rather than a saving: the article itself says so of the equipment. He voted for the 2026 budget, which raised the town-wide levy 7.74% with a tax-cap override (each 5–0, November 18, 2025), and now says “However, we did not need to have the tax increase that we did last year”. If he wins, he would be Supervisor in 2027, the year the new estimates are tested.

For Halpin, the incumbent

  • Could help. The amendments start from his Tentative, which the Riverhead News-Review reported “would end four-year streak of tax cap overrides”, and change its levy by about 1%. No one voted against them, and he voted for Amendment #5. He made the case for caution at the meeting, as reported: “This type of budgeting can come back to bite us in years ahead.” There is a basis for it: estimates closer to collections leave less room for error, and the deferred purchases still have to be paid for in 2027.
  • Could hurt. A council majority amending his budget in an election season casts his proposal as higher than it needed to be, and RiverheadLOCAL reports that he abstained on five of the six. He called the amendments politically motivated, but opponents could describe the abstentions as declining to back a lower levy.

Does the staff’s party matter?

The Supervisor picks his own office staff, and the Board acknowledges them by resolution; it appoints other staff itself, down to an accountant (Resolution 2026-891, October 6, 2026). Halpin kept the senior staff of the office he took over: the deputy supervisor and the chief of staff, whom RiverheadLOCAL titles chief of staff and budget officer. He added a legislative aide. He said his style is “evident in the people that I’ve carried over on my team,” RiverheadLOCAL reported the day before he took office. The Board acknowledged all three unanimously on January 6, 2026 (Resolutions 2026-58 to 2026-60).

The site’s editor reports that the staff of the Supervisor’s office are Republicans, the party of the council majority and of Rothwell. This site has not checked that against the Board of Elections’ enrollment records. Separately, the financial administrator discussed the Tentative’s figures when it was presented on September 24, as the Riverhead News-Review reported, and by Rothwell’s account helped shape the amendments, so the Town’s finance staff have advised both sides.

Whether it matters shows less in the numbers than in how the advice is received. Weighed evenly:

  • For Halpin, keeping his predecessor’s team bought experience and continuity in his first year, and fits his standing outside either party. But a Supervisor whose senior aides share his opponent’s party can be second-guessed, fairly or not, when the council majority rewrites his budget in an election season, starting from the Tentative his own office prepared.
  • For Rothwell, amendments built with the Town’s finance staff carry more weight than a campaign’s own figures. But critics can ask whether Town staff should be helping a candidate reshape his opponent’s budget weeks before the election, even though amending the budget is ordinary Board business.

Party is still not a test of the numbers: it says nothing about whether $1,000,000 of interest or $1,440,000 of building fees is a sound estimate. Rothwell says the financial administrator was comfortable with the amendments and the revenue assumptions behind them, and Town accounting staff prepared the fiscal impact statements on October 9, 2026. The Town has published no written forecast behind the new estimates. The surest way to take party out of it is to publish the assumptions, such as the interest rate and cash balance behind the interest estimate and the Building Department’s fee forecast, so residents can weigh the numbers rather than who supplied them.

Questions residents may want to ask

  • What interest rate and cash balance does the $1,000,000 General Fund interest estimate assume?
  • Building inspection fees came to $529,984 in the first half of 2026. What does the Building Department expect for 2027?
  • Which purchases would come back as separate resolutions using savings, when, and for how much? Will the Board list them with the adopted budget?
  • What change in CSEA dental plans is the $120,000 for, and what happens to the money if the change does not happen?
  • How would the Highway Department cover the $25,371 taken from its equipment budget and the $5,392 taken from snow removal?
  • The Preliminary Budget is due out on October 29. Will it show each amendment’s effect, and the equipment to be bought later from savings?
  • Will the Board keep all six when it adopts the budget after the election, and will the Supervisor vote for the adopted budget?

How this page was made

The six resolutions and their fiscal impact statements were read from the text of the October 15 work-session packet, whose pages are marked “Pre-Final Draft”, and checked against the page images. The votes are from the October 20 packet as the Town reposted it with results, and the check confirms that its six resolutions print the same accounts, amounts and reasons. A check runs on every build of this site. It confirms that each resolution balances fund by fund, that every account, amount and quotation appears on its packet page, and that each Tentative figure is the 2027 Budget Supplement’s.

The totals, rates and tax example are this site’s arithmetic. RiverheadLOCAL’s article is the source for who proposed the amendments and why, and its figures agree with the resolutions.
