2027 Spending Reduction

Riverhead’s 2027 budget is on track to pierce the state tax cap. Here’s the plainest way to close the gap — start with the three-number plan, then dig in as far as you like.

The problem

The 2027 budget is on track to blow past the tax cap by about $2,619,382

On current trends the tax levy would rise about 6% — but New York's cap allows only about 2%. To stay under the cap, the Town has to find roughly $2,619,382. The good news: it can be done with real, recurring savings — no reserve raid, no cap override. Here's how.

The unanimous retirement incentive plus only the firmest line trims cover essentially the entire gap. Everything below is the detail behind those two numbers — and the alternatives, for anyone who wants them.

1 · The retirement incentive

On July 7, 2026 the Board unanimously approved three voluntary retirement incentives (53 eligible). The Town projects $500,000–$800,000 in savings over the rest of 2026 and the full 2027 budget year — recurring payroll relief, exactly the kind of pressure the gap is made of.

Will it pass a divided board? The politicsOpen ▾

Closing the gap has to pass a Democratic Supervisor with a four-member Republican Council majority. Under NY Town Law the Supervisor prepares the budget and the Council adopts it, so a durable plan needs both. These levers are ordered by how well each survives that split — least partisan first.

1Bank the retirement-incentive savings the whole Board already voted forAlready agreed · 5–0
Closes: $500K–$800K recurring (Town projection)
The three union incentives passed 5–0 on July 7, 2026. Refilling the vacated posts at a lower step is the one salary saving both the Democratic Supervisor and the Republican majority have already endorsed — no new fight to have.
2Stack the sourced, audit-driven line trimsLow partisan friction
Closes: ~$2,095,931 in firm-confidence recurring trims
Each trim is tied to a specific, documented anomaly in the Town's own budget — a line that jumped 800%, 1,563%, or budgeted well above its own trailing actuals. Opposing one means defending an unexplained increase on the record, which is hard to do along party lines. These aren't program choices; they're accountability questions.
3Grow non-property-tax revenueNeutral · no service cut
Closes: $1 off the levy for every $1 of new state aid, fees, mortgage tax, or interest
Offsets the cap-busting levy dollar-for-dollar with no service cut and no tax increase — the rare move with nothing for either side to run against.
4Use a modest, disclosed one-time fund-balance appropriation for the residual onlyOne-time · bridge only
Closes: whatever gap remains after the recurring measures above
An easy vote — it raises no tax and cuts no service — but it spends one-time money on recurring cost, so it can only bridge a transitional remainder, not the whole gap. Appropriating the full $2.62M would burn ~8.8% of the $29.7M unassigned fund balance — the truly flexible cushion — for something that recurs.
5If the Board still wants the spending, override the cap — deliberately and in publicLegal if done in the open
Closes: the full gap, by raising the legal ceiling
The cap can be exceeded legally: adopt the override local law first, in the open, with the 60% vote on the record — as Riverhead did in 2023, 2024, and 2026. The problem to avoid is piercing the cap by accident; a disclosed, on-purpose override is a legitimate choice, not a violation.
6The blunt shortcut: an across-the-board 2.5% cutBlunt · overstated
Closes: ~$2.1M on paper
Politically tempting because it sounds even-handed, but it overstates what's actually cuttable — most of the base is personnel and mandated costs a flat directive can't touch — and it hits services indiscriminately. See the flat-cut table below.

The pragmatic reading: start with what already has bipartisan support (the 5–0 retirement incentive), stack the audit-driven trims and any non-tax revenue on top, and reserve one-time fund balance for the small residual. A cap override stays available, but as a deliberate, disclosed choice — not a number the budget backs into.

Board composition from the November 2025 results; budget roles per NY Town Law §§104–106. Cap-override mechanics per General Municipal Law §3-c (a 60% vote).

A flat 2.5% General Fund cut pencils out to about $1.73 million — nearly twice the $936,727 payroll-pressure gap. But roughly three-quarters of spending is personnel and mandated cost that a directive can’t touch; trim only the genuinely controllable lines and 2.5% yields about $726,000 — short of the gap on its own. That’s why the targeted, line-item package above (which reaches the controllable over-budget lines specifically) does more than a blunt across-the-board percentage.

“Controllable” excludes personnel and mandated costs (pension, debt service, insurance) a flat directive can't change.

Why you'll see two different “gap” numbersOpen ▾

The number on this page is the cap-piercing gap ($2,619,382) — how far the levy overshoots the 2% cap, and the one that forces a decision. You'll also see a smaller payroll-pressure gap ($936,727) — just the automatic wage growth needed to keep the same staff. The interactive package is measured against that smaller one, which is why it can read “fully covered” there while the bigger cap gap is the real target.

In real terms: inflation and buying powerOpen ▾

Most of the payroll-pressure gap isn't new programs — it's automatic cost-of-living growth (the model uses a 2.5% COLA). Meanwhile the tax cap limits levy growth to the lesser of 2% or inflation, so contracted costs rise about as fast as the revenue the Town is allowed to raise. Because prices keep rising, a line that merely holds flat in dollars is already a real cut in what it buys — so read this package in recurring, real terms: keeping recurring costs within recurring revenue, not a one-time patch.

Inflation: U.S. BLS Consumer Price Index. Levy limit: NY's 2% property-tax cap (lesser of 2% or CPI).
