Peconic Bay Community Preservation Fund

The 2% real-estate transfer tax that pays for land preservation — its revenue history, what it owes, and the real question behind talk of raising the rate.

Color an increase:
💡In plain English
Since 1999, the CPF has funded $76,983,250 of land purchases, protecting over 2,280 acres — but its transfer-tax revenue swung from $3,431,456 in 2019 to $9,539,252 in 2024, then pulled back 26.3% to $7,033,230 in 2025.
  • ›What the CPF is: a 2% tax on real-estate sales above $75K (unimproved) or $150K (improved), dedicated to farmland, open space, and historic-site preservation.
  • ›Why it swings: the tax is a cut of real-estate sale prices, so its revenue rises and falls with the housing market — not with anything the Town controls year to year.
  • ›This page: lays out the real numbers so residents can judge the volatility question themselves. It is not an argument for or against a rate increase.

2019 to 2024, transfer-tax revenue rose about 2.8x as the real-estate market ran hot; 2024 to 2025 it pulled back 26.3% as the market cooled. The fund balance kept growing through all of it, because the Town has been spending less than it takes in most years — but the revenue line itself has no floor built in.

Adopted: paying off the remaining preservation debt 5 years early

Adopted July 7, 2026

On July 7, 2026 the Town Board voted unanimously (Resolution 2026-642) to use $7,200,000 of CPF fund balance (plus about $92,000 from the general fund) to retire the remaining land-preservation debt — the borrowing that otherwise ran annually through 2030 — about 5 years early, saving an estimated $660,000 in future interest.

Is the current rate still enough?

There is no acute crisis in these numbers: the fund balance has grown every year shown here and the outstanding debt is shrinking each year. The real question is about reliability, not solvency — the CPF's only revenue source is a fixed 2% share of real-estate sale prices, which means every dollar of future land-preservation ambition or debt capacity rises and falls with a market the Town does not control. The 26.3% pullback from 2024 to 2025 happened without any change in Town policy — it was purely the market cooling.

Whether that argues for a higher rate, an expanded eligible-use list, or simply budgeting land purchases more conservatively in strong years to build a cushion for weak ones, is a genuine Town Board policy question. This page does not take a position on it — it lays out the real volatility so residents can weigh in with the actual numbers rather than a general impression that preservation funding is either flush or at risk.

The authority to levy this tax runs through 2050 (extended by referendum in 2016), and up to 20% of annual revenue may be used for water-quality projects rather than land purchases — both are facts about the program's current scope, not arguments either way on the rate.
