The Supervisor’s promises and the record

What Supervisor Jerry Halpin said he would do, what he says he has done, and what the Town’s own records show.

Independent project·Updated September 24, 2026·● Current·Check data freshness →
💡In plain English
Jerry Halpin became Town Supervisor in January 2026 and is on the ballot again on November 3, 2026. This page checks what he promised and what he says he has done against the Town’s own records. Of the claims on his campaign site, three hold up, four hold up in part and one isn’t a budget question. The biggest test is still ahead: his first budget, for 2027, which fills in below as soon as the Town publishes it.
  • ›Tentative budget: the Supervisor’s proposed budget for next year. The Town Board can change it before adopting the final budget by November 20.
  • ›Levy: the total amount the Town raises from property taxes.
  • ›Tax cap: the State’s limit on how much the levy can grow each year, usually about 2%. The Board can vote to go over it.
  • ›Fund balance: money left over from past years: the Town’s savings. Spending it helps one year’s taxes, but then it’s gone.
  • ›Roll call: how each of the five Town Board members voted.
How this page works

This page doesn’t endorse or oppose anyone, and it doesn’t judge anyone’s character or motives. It puts the Supervisor’s own words next to the Town’s own records. We never call a claim false. If the records back up only part of a claim, we say which part; if they can’t settle it, we say so. His opponent hasn’t served as Supervisor, so there’s no record of his to check here; both candidates’ plans are compared side by side on Candidate Proposals. Every vote on this page shows how all five Board members voted.

Background

  • He won by 37 votes after a hand recount, 3,958 to 3,921, defeating Supervisor Tim Hubbard (RiverheadLOCAL, Nov. 25, 2025).
  • He is serving a one-year term. New York moved local elections to even-numbered years, which cut his two-year term short, so the seat is on the ballot again this November (Riverhead News-Review, Feb. 2026).
  • He is an independent who ran on the Democratic line (East End Beacon, Dec. 31, 2025). He isn’t registered in any party, and he is the only Town Board member who isn’t a Republican (RiverheadLOCAL). In his words: “I am not a Republican, and I am not a Democrat” (votejerryhalpin.com). In February the Riverhead Town Democratic Committee unanimously backed him for reelection (Riverhead News-Review).
  • His opponent, Councilman Kenneth Rothwell, is one of the five Board members who will vote on the 2027 budget by November 20.

“Cut $40,000 from the salaries in the Supervisor’s Office.”
Partly supported

In short: Not from one year to the next. The office’s salaries are $3,018 less than in 2025, and counting a new health-insurance benefit the office costs about $11,500 more. Only a comparison with the 2026 budget reaches $40,000.

The Town’s 2026 pay list groups staff by department. Under “Supervisor’s Office” it lists a Deputy Town Supervisor, a Town Budget Officer and a Secretary (Resolution 2026-2). The 2025 list has the same three jobs. Counting his own pay, the office’s salaries total $355,834 in 2026, compared with $358,852 in 2025. That’s $3,018 less, not $40,000. He cut his own salary by $8,919, to $110,000. The budget allowed $118,919, what his predecessor was paid. Staff pay is $5,901 higher. The two senior staff he kept each got 3.275%, the same raise 16 other positions off the union pay scale got. His new aide took the Secretary’s job at the same salary as before. Salaries aren’t the whole picture. On December 16, 2025, before he took office, the Board voted to have the Town pay the full cost of health insurance for all three staff jobs, which had paid 25% themselves (Resolution 2025-984). The resolution says the Supervisor of the day suggested it “in lieu of merit increases.” It’s worth about $14,500 a year if all are enrolled, and it comes out of the Town’s health insurance budget, not the office’s. Counting it, the office costs about $11,500 more than in 2025. His aide’s contract includes the same fully paid coverage (Resolution 2026-58). Compared with the 2026 budget, which was passed before he took office, the salaries are $20,850 under the office’s full-time salary budget and $44,650 under its whole payroll budget of $400,484. The full-time budget also covers pay beyond base salaries; in 2025 the salaries came in $9,624 under it. The larger budget includes $20,000 for part-time help, and no one is on the pay list for it. Only that last comparison reaches $40,000. Money an office doesn’t spend isn’t a tax cut: whatever is left at the end of the year goes back into the Town’s savings. His 2027 proposal will show what he plans for the office; see “Did his own office’s payroll go down?” above.

“Closed the $450,000 shortfall in the 2026 budget left by the previous supervisor.”
Partly supported

In short: Partly. The records show one inherited shortfall, about $199,000 in the ambulance budget, and it was fixed. We found nothing showing the rest of the $450,000.

The records show one shortfall in the 2026 budget passed under his predecessor, and it was fixed: the Ambulance District’s payment to the Riverhead Volunteer Ambulance Corps (RVAC). The Board’s own resolutions say “several expenditure lines in the Adopted Budget were inaccurate,” and that RVAC’s contract (last year’s payment plus the tax-cap increase) needed more than the budget provided. Other things a Supervisor can do, and where each stands

Here are five other ways a Supervisor could act on taxes or on how the Town is run. For each: who decides, what has happened since January and what it’s worth. Only the first is one of his campaign claims. We don’t score these. Most need a Board majority, and the Supervisor has one vote of five.

Cut the payroll of his own office
Who decides: He proposes the office’s budget as the Town’s budget officer; the Board sets salaries by resolution.
  • He set his own salary at $110,000, $8,919 below the $118,919 the budget allowed.
  • The 2026 pay list, adopted unanimously at his first meeting on January 6, has the same three staff jobs in his office as 2025. It raised the Deputy Supervisor to $105,527 and the Town Budget Officer to $80,567, 3.275% each, and kept the Secretary’s job, now held by his Legislative Aide (Resolution 2026-58), at $59,740.
  • Counting his own pay, the office’s salaries are $355,834, $3,018 less than in 2025.
  • Since January 1 the Town also pays the staff’s 25% share of health insurance (Resolution 2025-984), about $14,500 a year if all are enrolled. That comes out of the Town’s health insurance budget, so it never shows up in the office’s budget. Counting it, the office costs about $11,500 more than in 2025.

What it’s worth: The office’s payroll budget is $400,484 in 2026, $376,684 of it for full-time salaries.

Choose his own senior staff
Who decides: The Supervisor appoints the Deputy Supervisor and his office staff; the Board formally acknowledges each appointment.
  • He kept both senior staff members from his predecessors. The Deputy Supervisor has held the job since 2020, under Supervisors Aguiar and Hubbard (Resolution 2020-130). The Town Budget Officer, who is also his Chief of Staff, has held the job since July 2022 (Resolution 2022-491). The Board acknowledged both on January 6 (Resolutions 2026-59 and 2026-60).
  • His one new hire is a Legislative Aide (Resolution 2026-58), who fills the office’s Secretary job. The 2026 pay list has the same three staff jobs in the office as 2025, so the office didn’t grow.
  • Keeping them gives a one-year term some continuity, since both have worked on the last four budgets. It also means his first budget comes from the same office that produced his predecessors’.

Have employees pay at least 15% of their health insurance
Who decides: The Board decides, by resolution, for elected officials and non-union managers. For union members the share is set by contract. The police unions’ contracts (PBA and SOA) end in 2026, so the new contracts are the chance to change it.
  • On December 16, 2025, before he took office, the Board voted unanimously to stop charging four appointed jobs their 25% share of medical, dental and vision insurance (Resolution 2025-984). Two of them are the Deputy Supervisor and the Chief of Staff, and the resolution calls it a change “in lieu of merit increases.” The 2026 pay list then raised both by 3.275%, the same raise it gave 16 other positions off the union pay scale.
  • No resolution passed in 2026 sets or restores an employee share.

What it’s worth: About $63,800 a year at 15% across the 22 senior-staff and elected positions we model, and about $19,300 for bringing back the 25% share on the four jobs alone. Both use the lowest State health plan (NYSHIP) rate, for one person, assuming everyone is enrolled. A 15% share for union members would save more, depending on what each contract charges now, which the budget doesn’t show.

Set a new rule for the Town’s savings
Who decides: The Board decides, by resolution. The Supervisor can propose one, and as budget officer he decides how much savings each budget proposal uses.
  • The Town’s current rule says General Fund savings should be at least 15% of the General Fund budget, with an upper target of 20%.
  • At the end of 2025 the audit found $29,671,084 in General Fund savings not set aside for anything. That’s 42.9% of the 2026 General Fund budget, more than twice the upper target. Resolutions passed in 2026 have committed $3,896,618 of it.
  • No resolution passed in 2026 adopts or changes the rule.

What it’s worth: At most $11,951,834 above the upper target after this year’s commitments. It’s one-time money: it can pay off debt, pay for building projects or soften one year’s taxes, but it can’t cover an ongoing cost for long.

Adopt a housing plan
Who decides: The Board passes local laws and a community housing plan. A community housing fund also needs a town-wide vote (New York Town Law § 64-k).
  • In March the Board replaced the Town’s accessory-apartment law with a new accessory dwelling unit (ADU) law (Resolution 2026-252, unanimous).
  • Riverhead is still the only one of the five Peconic Bay towns without a community housing plan or fund. No resolution passed in 2026 starts either one.

What it’s worth: A 0.5% housing transfer tax would have raised an estimated $5,479,900 to $5,925,500 from 2023 through 2025, based on the Town’s own audited transfer-tax revenue.

Smart politics, or a missed chance?

Our scorecard, above, answers the narrower question: how much of the restraint he promised shows up in his budget, scored the same way as the three before it. Whether it’s smart politics is for voters to judge. What the records can do is set the choices in his budget next to what he said he would do. He named the tension himself before taking office: “Our workers want to be paid better and our taxpayers want to have a lessened tax burden” (East End Beacon). Those two goals can pull against each other. Using savings to hold taxes down can be good politics in an election year and a problem for the 2028 budget. Raising taxes to pay for ongoing costs can be sound finance and costly at the polls.

  • His budget is a proposal, not the final word. The Board adopts the budget by November 20, after the November 3 election, and four of its five members are Republicans, including his opponent. In the 5 years we’ve checked, the Board adopted the proposal unchanged 3 times and never moved total spending by more than 0.72%, so the September 24 proposal usually becomes the budget.
  • Some of the 2027 pressure is outside any Supervisor’s control. The police unions’ contracts (PBA and SOA) end in 2026, and no one knows yet what the new ones will cost. The July 7 agreements with both unions set up the retirement incentive, not new contracts. Pension and health costs are set by the State and by insurers.
  • The 2026 budget he inherited raised town-wide taxes by the same measure he campaigned against, and it was adopted the month he won. The 2027 proposal is the first one that’s his.

The evidence is in the tables above: taxes against the cap, against the increase he ran on, the use of savings, what departments asked for and his own office’s payroll, plus where each of the other options stands. Our own measure is the scorecard.

How we rate claims

  • Supported by the records: the Town’s records show what the claim says.
  • Partly supported: the records back up part of it, and the finding says which part.
  • Not in the records we check: the records we check neither confirm nor contradict it. That isn’t evidence against the claim.
  • Not a budget or voting question: not a budget or voting matter, so we don’t rate it.
