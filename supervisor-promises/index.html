The Supervisor’s promises and the record

What Supervisor Jerry Halpin said he would do and says he has done, checked against records the Town itself publishes — and the tests his first budget, the 2027 Tentative, will answer.

Independent project·Updated September 23, 2026·● Current·Check data freshness →
What this page is, and is not.

It is not an endorsement or an opposition, and it does not judge character or intent. It sets the Supervisor’s own published words beside the Town’s own records. It never calls a statement false: where the record contradicts part of a claim it says which part, and where the record cannot settle a claim it says that. He is on the November 3, 2026 ballot; his opponent has no record as Supervisor to check, so both platforms are weighed evenly on Candidate Proposals. Every vote below shows the full roll call.

Context

  • Won by 37 votes after a manual recount, 3,958 to 3,921, unseating Supervisor Tim Hubbard (RiverheadLOCAL, Nov. 25, 2025).
  • Serving a one-year term: New York’s move to even-year local elections cut his two-year term short, so the seat is on the ballot again this November (Riverhead News-Review, Feb. 2026).
  • An independent who ran on the Democratic line (East End Beacon, Dec. 31, 2025) and is not registered in any party, the only member of the Town Board who is not a Republican (RiverheadLOCAL). “I am not a Republican, and I am not a Democrat” (votejerryhalpin.com). In February the Riverhead Town Democratic Committee unanimously backed him for reelection (Riverhead News-Review).
  • His opponent, Councilman Kenneth Rothwell, is one of the five votes that will adopt the 2027 budget, on or before November 20.

“Cut $40,000 from the salaries in the Supervisor’s Office.”
Partly supported

The salary schedule adopted at his first meeting lists the office’s staff under its own heading, Supervisor’s Office: a Deputy Town Supervisor, a Town Budget Officer and a Secretary (Resolution 2026-2). The 2025 schedule has the same three titles. With his own salary, the office’s salaries come to $355,834 for 2026 against $358,852 for 2025: $3,018 less, not $40,000. His own is $8,919 lower: $110,000, against the $118,919 budgeted for the post and paid to his predecessor. The staff’s are $5,901 higher: the two appointees he kept each received 3.275%, the rate the same schedules gave 16 other positions paid off the union grid, and his appointee took the Secretary’s post at its 2025 salary. Salaries are not all of it. Resolution 2025-984, adopted on December 16, 2025, before he took office, at the suggestion of the Supervisor of the day and “in lieu of merit increases,” made health premiums fully employer paid from January 1 for all three staff titles, which had paid 25%. At the lowest (individual) NYSHIP rate that is about $14,500 a year if all are enrolled, charged to the Town’s health insurance line rather than the office’s; counted in, the office costs about $11,500 more than in 2025. His Legislative Aide’s agreement carries the same 100% employer-paid coverage (Resolution 2026-58). Against the 2026 budget, adopted before he took office, the salaries are $20,850 below the office’s full-time salary line, which also pays earnings beyond base salary (the 2025 salaries were $9,624 below 2025’s), and $44,650 below its whole personal-services budget of $400,484, which includes $20,000 for part-time help the schedule lists no one for. Only that last comparison reaches $40,000. The 2027 Tentative will show what he proposes for the office’s payroll line; see “Did the payroll for his own office go down?” above.

“Closed the $450,000 shortfall in the 2026 budget left by the previous supervisor.”
Partly supported

The Town’s records show one shortfall in the 2026 budget adopted under his predecessor, and it was closed: the Ambulance District’s payment to the Riverhead Volunteer Ambulance Corps (RVAC). The Board’s own resolutions say that “several expenditure lines in the Adopted Budget were inaccurate” and that RVAC’s contract, which entitles it to the previous year’s payment plus the tax-cap increase, required more than the budget provided. The two corrections added $199,322 to RVAC’s line: $119,322 moved from other ambulance lines and $80,000 from the district’s fund balance. That is less than half of $450,000, and it sits in the Ambulance District’s budget, not the General Fund. No resolution, budget document or audit this site reads identifies the rest; every 2026 agenda packet through September 15 was searched for corrections to the adopted budget. The year’s other budget changes pay for needs that arose during the year, such as “unanticipated” legal work and road salt after the winter storms, or for new initiatives, and none says the adopted budget was short. Separately, the 2025 audit reports deficits of $692,688 in the Recreation Program Fund and $73,185 in the Police Athletic League Fund at the end of 2025, which it expects to be eliminated in 2026; whether they were will show in the 2026 audit.

Other levers, and where each stands

Five levers a Supervisor can reach for, set against the record since January: who can act, what has happened so far, and what each is worth. Only the first appears among his campaign’s own claims. None is scored. Most need a Board majority, and the Supervisor is one of five votes.

Reduce the payroll of the Supervisor’s own office
Who can act: The Supervisor proposes the office’s budget line as budget officer; the Board sets salaries by resolution.
  • His own salary was set at $110,000, $8,919 below the $118,919 budgeted for the position.
  • The 2026 salary schedule, adopted unanimously at his first meeting on January 6, lists the same three staff titles under Supervisor’s Office as 2025. It raised the Deputy Supervisor to $105,527 and the Town Budget Officer to $80,567, 3.275% each, and kept the Secretary’s post, filled by his Legislative Aide (Resolution 2026-58), at $59,740.
  • With his own salary, the office’s salaries are $355,834, $3,018 less than in 2025.
  • From January 1, Resolution 2025-984 also moved the staff’s 25% share of health premiums to the Town, about $14,500 a year at the individual rate if all are enrolled. It is charged to the Town’s health insurance line, so it never appears in the office’s budget line; counted in, the office costs about $11,500 more than in 2025.

What it is worth: The office’s personal-services line is $400,484 in the 2026 budget, $376,684 of it for full-time salaries.

Appoint his own senior staff
Who can act: The Supervisor appoints the Deputy Supervisor and his confidential staff; the Board acknowledges each appointment.
  • He kept both senior appointees of his predecessors: the Deputy Supervisor, in that post since 2020 under Supervisors Aguiar and Hubbard (terms set by Resolution 2020-130), and the Town Budget Officer, who is also his Chief of Staff, in that post since July 2022 (terms set by Resolution 2022-491). The Board acknowledged both on January 6 (Resolutions 2026-59 and 2026-60).
  • His one new appointment is a Legislative Aide (Resolution 2026-58), who fills the office’s Secretary post: the 2026 schedule lists the same three staff titles under Supervisor’s Office as 2025, so the office did not grow.
  • Keeping them brings continuity to a one-year term, since both held their posts through the last four budgets. It also means his first Tentative comes from the same office that produced his predecessors’.
Records: Authorized salary schedules, 2022–2026; 2020–2025 Town payroll; TB Resolutions 2024-35 and 2024-36 (citing 2020-130 and 2022-491); TB Resolutions 2026-58, 2026-59, 2026-60
Require employees to pay at least 15% of their health premiums
Who can act: The Board, by resolution, for elected officials and managers outside a union. For union members the share is set by contract; the PBA and SOA contracts expire at the end of 2026, so their successors are the opening.
  • On December 16, 2025, before he took office, the Board moved four appointed titles, including the Deputy Supervisor and the Chief of Staff, from paying 25% of their medical, dental and vision premiums to paying nothing, “in lieu of merit increases” (Resolution 2025-984, unanimous). The 2026 salary schedule then raised both, by the 3.275% it gave 16 other positions paid off the union grid.
  • No resolution adopted in 2026 sets or restores an employee contribution.

What it is worth: About $63,800 a year at 15% across the 22 senior-staff and elected positions this site models, and about $19,300 for restoring 25% on the four titles alone. Both use the lowest (individual) NYSHIP rate at full enrollment. A 15% share for union members would save more, by an amount that depends on what each contract charges now, which the budget does not print.

Adopt a new fund balance policy
Who can act: The Board, by resolution. The Supervisor can propose one, and as budget officer decides how much of the balance each Tentative spends.
  • The Town’s policy sets a floor of 15% of General Fund appropriations and an upper target of 20%.
  • The audited unassigned General Fund balance at the end of 2025 was $29,671,084, 42.9% of the 2026 General Fund budget, more than twice the upper target. Resolutions adopted in 2026 have committed $4,821,618 of it.
  • No resolution adopted in 2026 adopts or amends the policy.

What it is worth: At most $11,026,834 above the upper target after this year’s commitments. It is one-time money: it can retire debt, fund capital or soften a single year’s levy, but it cannot carry a recurring cost for long.

Adopt a housing policy
Who can act: The Board adopts local laws and a community housing plan. A community housing fund also needs a townwide referendum (New York Town Law § 64-k).
  • In March the Board replaced the Town Code’s accessory-apartment law with a new accessory dwelling unit (ADU) law (Resolution 2026-252, unanimous).
  • Riverhead remains the only one of the five Peconic Bay towns without a community housing plan or fund. No resolution adopted in 2026 starts either.

What it is worth: A 0.5% housing transfer tax would have raised an estimated $5,479,900 to $5,925,500 from 2023 through 2025, from the Town’s own audited transfer-tax revenue.

The 2% line is the reference the site uses everywhere. It is stricter than the legal levy limit, which the Town does not print (Tax Cap). Every other threshold is “no worse than the year before.” Any change to these criteria will be dated here, with the earlier scores kept.

Politically prudent — or a missed opportunity?

The site’s scoring rule, below the levers, answers the narrower half: how much of the restraint he promised the Tentative delivers, scored the same way as the three Tentatives before it. Whether that was politically prudent is a judgment for voters. What the record can do is show the choices the Tentative made, against what the Supervisor said he would do. He named the tension himself before taking office: “Our workers want to be paid better and our taxpayers want to have a lessened tax burden” (East End Beacon). The two questions can pull against each other: a Tentative that holds the levy down by drawing on fund balance can be good politics in an election year and a problem for the 2028 budget, while one that raises the levy to fund recurring costs can be sound finance and costly at the polls.

  • The Tentative is a proposal. The Board adopts the budget by November 20, after the November 3 election, and four of its five members are Republicans, including the Supervisor’s opponent. On the record so far the Board adopted the Tentative unchanged in 3 of 5 years and never moved total appropriations by more than 0.72%, so the September 24 document tends to be the budget.
  • Part of the 2027 pressure is outside any Supervisor’s control. The PBA and SOA contracts expire at the end of 2026 and what their successors will cost is not yet known; the July 7 stipulations with both unions set up the retirement incentive, not new contracts. Pension and health costs are set by the State and by carriers.
  • The 2026 budget he inherited raised the town-wide levy on the same measure he campaigned against, and was adopted the month he won. The 2027 Tentative is the first one that is his.

The evidence is the table above: the levy against the cap, against the increase he ran on, the use of one-time money, what departments asked for and his own office’s payroll, and where each of the other levers stands. The site’s own measure of it is the scoring rule.

How claims are marked

  • Supported by the record — the Town’s records show what the claim says.
  • Partly supported — the records show part of it; the finding says which part.
  • Not in the records this site reads — the records this site reads neither confirm nor contradict it. That is not evidence against the claim.
  • Outside this site’s fiscal records — not a budget or voting matter, so not assessed here.

Items marked “as reported” are someone else’s account of his campaign, not his own words; each names whose account it is. Roll calls are from the Town Board minutes and, where the minutes omit them, the official Agenda Packet.
