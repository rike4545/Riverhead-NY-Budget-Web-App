Police spending and reported crime

What the Town appropriates for its Police Department, next to the Index Crime the department reports to New York State — shown as two series, not one line arguing with itself.

Independent project·Updated September 26, 2026·● Current·Check data freshness →
💡In plain English
Between 2021 and 2024, Riverhead’s Police appropriation rose +19% and the Index Crime the department reported rose +25% — almost all of it property crime, and almost all of that larceny. Violent crime over the same years moved -3%. That is the short view, and on its own it misleads. Over the full series the direction is the other way: Riverhead recorded 1,908 index crimes in 2000 and 955 in 2024 — 50% of the peak. Today's count is a recovery from a pandemic trough inside a long decline, not a break from it.
  • ›Index Crime: the FBI’s seven-offence definition — murder, rape, robbery, aggravated assault, burglary, larceny, motor-vehicle theft. It is what New York State publishes by agency, and it is far from everything a police department does.
  • ›Self-reported: the counts come from the department itself. A change in how incidents are recorded moves the line without anything changing on the street.
  • ›Adopted, not spent: the dollars are adopted appropriations. Police overtime in particular routinely runs above the adopted line.
  • ›Why no single chart: dollars and crime counts have nothing to do with each other numerically. Drawing them on one axis with two scales is the oldest way to make two unrelated lines look related, so this page does not.

Read this before the charts

Neither line explains the other, and the page does not suggest it does. Spending buys staffing, contractual raises and equipment; reported crime moves with what happens and with what gets reported. A department that prevents a crime records fewer of them, not more — so a falling count is not a failing department and a rising one is not a failing budget. What the pair does answer is narrower and still worth asking: what has the Town been paying, what has it been recording, and how does that sit next to the neighbours who publish the same two numbers.

The budget runs ahead of the crime data. 2026 is adopted at $22,388,906; the State’s series ends at 2024, so the last 2 budget years have no crime figure to sit beside yet.

In 2024, 859 of the 955 index crimes were larceny — 90% of the total. Violent offences were 37. When this total moves, larceny is usually what moved.

Why Riverhead has a police budget at all

Riverhead polices itself because it declined to hand the job to the county. After New York passed the 1958 legislation creating Suffolk's county-executive government, a referendum put a county police force to the towns. The five western towns — Babylon, Huntington, Islip, Smithtown, Brookhaven — voted to join. The five eastern towns — Riverhead, Southold, Shelter Island, East Hampton, Southampton — kept their own. The Suffolk County Police Department began operating on January 1, 1960, and the split has held ever since.

Police is the largest single department in Riverhead’s General Fund. That it sits in the town budget at all — rather than in a county police district tax, as it does for most of Suffolk’s population — is a structural decision taken around 1960 and never revisited. It is also why this page compares Riverhead only with the other four East End towns: they are not the nearest neighbours, they are the entire set of Suffolk towns that still have a town police line to compare.

Secondary accounts differ on whether the referendum itself fell in 1958 or 1959; they agree on the 1958 enabling legislation and on the department beginning operations January 1, 1960, so this page states those and not a contested referendum date. The record that would settle it is the Suffolk County Charter’s police-district provisions and the referendum return itself, neither of which is published in a machine-readable form.

Read these as counts, not as risk. The East End towns differ substantially in size, and a bar that is twice as long can mean twice the crime, twice the people, or twice the reporting. Turning counts into a rate per 10,000 residents needs Census population, which this build did not have — the parser computes it automatically the moment the key is available, rather than this page carrying a population figure typed in by hand.

Left out for incomplete reporting: East Hampton Town PD (9 months). Their totals are real but not comparable to a full year, and scaling them up would be inventing data.

What the Board sees, and in what form

The Police Department reports to the Board monthly and the Justice Court, Code Enforcement and Town Clerk report alongside it. Those monthly figures are published as scanned images inside the agenda packet, with no text layer, so they cannot be read by software — including the Town’s own. This page therefore uses the State’s annual series, which is machine-readable and comparable across departments.

What this cannot tell you

  • Index crime is the FBI's seven-offence definition. It excludes most of what a police department spends its time on — traffic, quality-of-life calls, mental-health response, overdoses, domestic incidents that do not reach the aggravated-assault threshold.
  • The counts are self-reported by the agency. A change in reporting practice moves the line without anything changing on the street.
  • Appropriations are adopted budget, not actual spending. Police overtime in particular routinely runs above the adopted line.
  • Neither series is adjusted for inflation or for the summer population swing, which is large on the East End and falls on the same department.
  • A ratio of dollars to reported crimes is an arithmetic fact, not a productivity measure. A department that prevents a crime records fewer, not more.
  • Peer agencies are compared on raw counts unless a Census population is available. The East End towns differ substantially in size, so a count says how much a department recorded, not how much risk a resident faces.

Police appropriations come from the Town's own adopted budgets, summed across every account-level line item in the Police department. Reported crime is the State's Index Crime count for Riverhead Town PD, self-reported by the department to DCJS. The two are joined on year and nothing is inferred from the pairing: spending buys staffing, equipment and contractual raises, and reported crime moves with what happens and with what gets reported.
