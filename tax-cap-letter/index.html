The supervisors’ tax-cap letter, checked against Riverhead’s record

On October 5, 2026, Suffolk town supervisors, Riverhead’s among them, asked Albany to rethink the property tax cap. This page sets out what they asked, where their case is strong, what it leaves out, and what Riverhead’s own budgets and audits show.

Independent project·Updated October 9, 2026·Current·Check data freshness →

In plain English

Six Suffolk supervisors signed a letter asking the State to change how the property tax cap treats costs such as pensions, health insurance and garbage, and to fund the mandates it passes down. Their core complaint holds up in Riverhead’s own numbers. But the letter, as described in public, is a request for a conversation rather than a proposal, and it leaves out what a resident would need to judge it.
  • The real problem: inflation for 2027 is 3.13%, but the cap’s growth factor stops at 2%, and Riverhead’s pension and health-insurance lines are rising faster than either.
  • What is missing: the letter, as reported, puts no formula or price on what it wants, and does not weigh the override towns already have, their reserves, or the costs towns set themselves.
  • Riverhead: the Board overrode the cap unanimously for four budgets in a row, and the General Fund ended 2025 with $28,829,513 of unassigned fund balance. The Supervisor says the 2027 Tentative is within its limit.

What the supervisors asked

At a Suffolk County Supervisors Association press conference at Brookhaven Town Hall on October 5, six of the county’s ten town supervisors signed a letter to Governor Hochul and state lawmakers. Brookhaven’s Supervisor said the other four agree with it. This site has not found the letter itself published, so this page relies on the two public accounts of it.

Riverhead’s Supervisor, Jerry Halpin, was there. RiverheadLOCAL reported that he “highlighted the contradiction between being asked to be a “pro-housing community” without being given the tools to deliver the services needed to sustain it.” He said: “We’re just asking Albany to help us to continue that conversation, to sit down with us and figure this out.”

Where their case is strong

The cap stops at 2% even when inflation does not

Under General Municipal Law §3-c, the levy’s growth factor is the lesser of 2% and inflation. It can never be more than 2%. For 2027 the State Comptroller put inflation at 3.13%, and the Comptroller’s office says: “For the sixth consecutive year, the allowable tax levy growth will be limited to 2%.” In a year like that, a town that holds to the cap is raising less than prices rose.

Pension and health costs are rising faster

The State Comptroller, who runs the pension system, raised employers’ rates for the bills towns pay in 2027: “Employers’ average contribution rates will increase from 16.5% to 17.6% of payroll for the Employees’ Retirement System (ERS) and from 33.7% to 36.5% of payroll for the Police and Fire Retirement System (PFRS).” The cap only excludes the part of a rate increase above two percentage points. As RiverheadLOCAL summarised the Comptroller’s list: “For 2027 town budgets, the state comptroller lists no exclusion for the Employees’ Retirement System. It lists a 0.8% payroll exclusion for Police and Fire Retirement System payments made in February, but none for those made in December.”

Riverhead’s own figures show the pressure. The audited General Fund spent $16,384,924 on employee benefits in 2024 and $18,829,792 in 2025, up 14.9% in a year. In the 2027 Tentative, the General Fund’s five pension and health-insurance lines grow by $1,473,401. The whole General Fund levy rises $1,472,341. On their own, those five lines take up about the entire increase. The levy is not the General Fund’s only source, though: its other revenues rise $1,728,862 in the Tentative, more than the levy does.

Riverhead’s own budget letters have said the same. The 2025 Tentative’s: “The Town-wide 2025 Budget is 4.14% over the tax cap, due in large part to increases to insurance premiums and retirement systems contributions.”

Towns have no capital exclusion

Islip’s Supervisor pointed to school districts, which can leave the tax needed for eligible capital spending, including school debt, out of the cap. RiverheadLOCAL notes that towns have no comparable exclusion, even for borrowing voters approved.

Some costs come from Albany

Brookhaven’s Supervisor gave the State’s cannabis licensing as an example of work the State hands towns with no fee to pay for it. The accounts give no dollar figure for it, or for any other mandate.

What the letter leaves out

A formula, and any numbers

Neither account reports a replacement formula, a percentage, which costs should be excluded and by how much, or what any change would add to a tax bill. The SCSA’s own statement asks for “a constructive meeting” to “discuss potential adjustments.” Even the goal is unsettled: RiverheadLOCAL quotes Huntington’s Supervisor hoping the State will “adjust” the 2% cap; the statement printed by Long Island Life & Politics and Patch has him hoping it will “abandon” it.

The override towns already have

The cap is not a hard limit. A town board can go above it by first passing a local law with at least 60% of the board’s votes: three of Riverhead’s five. Riverhead’s Board did so for each of the last four budgets, every time unanimously:

The override is a recorded vote, and a public one. A looser formula would let levies rise without it. The letter, as reported, does not say why the override is not enough. For a town, the “vote” Senator Palumbo mentions is the Town Board’s: school districts need 60% of voters, towns need 60% of the board.

Reserves

Riverhead’s General Fund ended 2025 with $28,829,513 of unassigned fund balance, according to its audit: 0.4% of the $69,113,159 2026 General Fund budget. After the $4,951,618 the Board committed by resolution during 2026, at most $23,877,895 is left, 0.3%. The Town’s own policy floor is 0.15%. Reserves are one-time money and cannot pay a cost that recurs every year. But they matter to how urgent the problem is, and the letter, as reported, does not mention them.

The costs a town sets itself

The State sets health premiums, but each town decides how much of them its employees pay. On December 16, 2025, Riverhead’s Board voted 5–0 (Resolution 2025-984) to make the premiums fully employer-paid for four management titles that had paid 25% of them, a change the resolution says was suggested “in lieu of merit increases.” Salaries, staffing and contracts are local decisions too. Even on pensions, the Comptroller lists “higher salaries, plan options selected by employers, recent legislative reforms to Tiers 5 and 6 and member retirement rates” among what drives the rates.

Riverhead’s 2027 Tentative shows costs can be managed locally. The Supervisor’s letter: “This year, we were able to offer a retirement incentive that yielded a $819,000 savings directly to the General Fund for 2027.”

The pension picture is mixed

For the bills towns will pay in 2028, the Comptroller lowered the civilian rate from 17.6% to 17.3% of payroll, while the police and fire rate rises from 36.5% to 37.4%. The supervisors cited the previous year’s increases.

Garbage, in Riverhead

Riverhead pays for garbage collection through its Refuse and Garbage District, and the 2027 Tentative raises all of that district’s revenue from property taxes: $5,254,540 in 2026, $5,302,990 proposed for 2027, up $48,450 (0.9%). Disposal costs may well rise when the Brookhaven ashfill closes, but that is not in Riverhead’s 2027 budget.

Where it reads as rhetoric

Some of what was said does not match the record. These are likely concerns for residents, not findings about anyone’s motives.

The timing also matters. The appeal was made as towns prepare their 2027 budgets, which Riverhead must adopt by November 20 (Town Law §109). The Legislature assembles each January (State Constitution, Article XIII, §4), so in the normal course no change could reach a 2027 tax bill. Whatever comes of the letter, this year’s budget rests on choices the Board makes now.

What a complete proposal would show

A formula
The growth factor or exclusion it wants, written so the Comptroller could calculate it, and what it would have allowed in each of the last few years.
Each town’s numbers
Its levy limit, the costs it says are above it, and how much of each cost it sets itself, such as salaries, staffing and the share of health premiums employees pay.
Reserves and results
Each town’s unassigned fund balance against its own policy, and whether recent budgets ended in surplus or deficit.
Why the override is not enough
Towns can already go above the limit with a 60% vote of the board. A change to the formula would let levies rise without that recorded vote.
The effect on a tax bill
What the change would add to a typical homeowner’s bill, town by town.
The mandates, priced
Which state requirements cost what, so Albany can fund them or end them, which is the test the local Assembly Member set.

Questions residents could ask

  1. Of the increases in the 2027 Tentative, which does the Town count as imposed by the State, and which are its own decisions?
  2. In dollars, what would the formula the supervisors want have added to Riverhead’s 2027 levy limit?
  3. Will the Town publish its tax-cap filing, the calculation behind the 2.79% limit in the Supervisor’s letter?
  4. The General Fund ended 2025 with more unassigned fund balance than its policy requires. How much of it is planned for one-time costs, and when?
  5. Would the Town keep holding a public override vote for any levy above a new, higher limit?

What this page does not say

This is an independent reading of public records, not a legal or audit finding. The letter itself has not been published; its asks are quoted from the two accounts linked below, and the quotes are as those outlets reported them. The other towns’ figures are the supervisors’ own and are not checked here:

  • Smithtown’s Supervisor said the cap lets his town raise taxes by $1.6 million in 2027, and that fixed costs already put the increase about $1.2 million over that.
  • Islip’s Supervisor said a new garbage contract cost her town $6 million more than the last one.
  • Brookhaven’s Supervisor said waste disposal is “going to be the single largest expense”: the Brookhaven ashfill is expected to close in 2028, with no settled plan for the ash after that.

Riverhead’s 2.79% limit is the Supervisor’s figure; the calculation behind it is in the Town’s filing with the State Comptroller, which the budget does not print.
