The rules, and how to read a filing

What a donor may give in a Riverhead race, and what each part of a committee's report means.

What contribution limits apply?

Riverhead Town races in 2026, from the County's own index

New York Election Law § 14-114 sets rules that depend on the race, donor relationship, and applicable election cycle. The Suffolk County Board of Elections sets each town's limits every year from its voter counts. Its 2026 index gives these for the Town of Riverhead:

General election
Individual: $1,219.30
Family: $6,096.50
Democratic primary
Individual: $1,000.00
Family: $1,685.75
Republican primary
Individual: $1,000.00
Family: $2,148.50

A limit is per donor and per election, and some donors (a candidate's own money, family members) follow different rules. Before reading a gift as over the limit, check the filing itself: whether it is one gift or several, for the primary or the general, and from whom. Source: Suffolk County Board of Elections, "Campaign Contribution Limit Index for 2026" (April 23, 2026).

  • Most individual donors: The ordinary limit is generally based on registered voters in the district × $0.05, subject to a $1,000 minimum.
  • Qualifying family donors: Certain close family members may be subject to a separate higher limit based on registered voters × $0.25, subject to a $1,250 minimum.
  • Candidate's own money: A candidate's personal contributions are generally not subject to the ordinary contribution cap for local town races. Different rules can apply under the State public campaign-financing program.

Money, in-kind and LLC gifts: how a filing shows them

A committee lists every contribution on a lettered schedule. The letter tells you what kind of gift it was and who gave it, so it is the first thing to check when a donor and a filing seem to tell different stories. The schedule names below are the State Board of Elections' own, as the open data prints them.

Schedule A
“Monetary Contributions Received From Ind. & Part.”

Money from a person or a partnership: cash, check, card or an online payment.

Schedule B
“Monetary Contributions Received From Corporation”

Money from a corporation's own funds. A business owner's personal gift belongs on Schedule A instead.

Schedule C
“Monetary Contributions Received From All Other”

Money from anyone else, such as a limited liability company (LLC), a union or a political committee.

Schedule D
“In-Kind (Non-Monetary) Contributions Received”

Goods or services given to the campaign instead of money, such as printing, food or work, recorded at what they were worth. A discount the business does not give everyone generally counts too, at the amount taken off. In-kind gifts count toward the same limits as money.

Schedule O
“LLCs/Partnership/Subcontractor”

Who stands behind a gift from an LLC or partnership: the filing splits the amount among its members, so the same gift appears here as well as on Schedule C.

When a donor's account and the filing differ

A donor may describe a gift one way while the committee reported it another way: a discount filed as a check, or a personal gift filed as a business's. Neither is proof of wrongdoing, but the public record should match what happened.

  • Check the letter and the payment: a gift filed on Schedule A, B or C with a check number was reported as money. A discount or donated work should be on Schedule D, valued at what it was worth.
  • Ask the treasurer: the committee, not the donor, files the report. If it is wrong, the committee's treasurer can file an amended report, and the open data marks amended reports.
  • Not a finding: a mismatch is usually a bookkeeping error, and the State Board of Elections, not this site, decides whether a filing breaks the rules.

Sources, coverage & how to read this page

The page uses New York State's open campaign-finance data from data.ny.gov, with a local snapshot for initial display and live refresh controls for updated records. Coverage shown here is 2005–2026.

  • Candidate registrations and committee/filer records are separate records and may have different statuses.
  • Where historical committees are combined, the page notes the scope so a committee total is not mistaken for a single election total.
  • Missing activity in a snapshot does not necessarily mean no contribution occurred; check live data and source filings.
  • Employee-donor matches rely on names and can produce false positives or miss name variations. They are not findings of misconduct.
  • Contribution-limit figures are dated references. Current compliance requires the current limits and complete filing context.
