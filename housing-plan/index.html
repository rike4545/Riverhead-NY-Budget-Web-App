The Community Housing Plan Riverhead never wrote

State law gives the five Peconic Bay towns the same tool: a half-percent transfer tax dedicated to housing, unlocked only after the town adopts a community housing plan by local law. Shelter Island adopted its plan in 2023. So did the other three. Riverhead has neither the plan nor the fund — and this page puts a number on that, using the Town's own audited transfer-tax record.

What the law actually requires

The Peconic Bay Region Community Housing Act (New York Town Law § 64-k, enacted 2021 and amended 2022) names East Hampton, Riverhead, Shelter Island, Southampton, Southold — all five Peconic Bay towns, Riverhead included. It is not a Hamptons-only statute. Six steps stand between a town and a spendable housing fund, and only one of them belongs to the voters:

1The Town Board adopts a local law establishing the fundSTATUTE

A town in the Peconic Bay region may adopt a local law imposing a supplemental 0.5% real-estate transfer tax dedicated to community housing. Nothing happens without this first vote — which is the step Riverhead's board declined to take in 2022.

2The voters decide, at a mandatory referendumSTATUTEVOTERS DECIDE

The local law is subject to a mandatory referendum. In November 2022 voters in East Hampton, Southampton, Southold, and Shelter Island approved theirs. Riverhead residents were never asked, because the question never reached the ballot.

3The Town Board creates an advisory boardSTATUTE

A town that establishes a fund "shall create an advisory board to review and make recommendations regarding the town's community housing plan." Its seats are specified by statute, not left to the board's discretion.

4The Town Board adopts the community housing plan — by local lawSTATUTE

This is the requirement the page is named for, and it is a precondition on spending, not on collecting.

“Before a town in the Peconic Bay region may expend any funds pursuant to this section, the town board shall first adopt a town housing plan which establishes an implementation plan for the provision of community housing opportunities by the fund”— New York Town Law § 64-k
5The plan has to follow smart-growth principlesSTATUTE

The statute names them: account for and minimize the social, economic, and environmental costs of new development — infrastructure, sewers and wastewater, water, schools, recreation, loss of open space and farmland — and encourage development where transportation, water, and sewer infrastructure are already available or practical.

6Only then can the money be spent, on statutory usesSTATUTE

Eligible expenses run to land acquisition, planning, engineering, and construction costs, plus other hard and soft costs directly related to the construction, rehabilitation, purchase, or rental of community housing.

Who sits on the advisory board that reviews the plan

The statute specifies the seats — a town cannot staff it with insiders alone:

  • A representative of the construction industry
  • A representative of the real-estate industry
  • A representative of the banking industry
  • Three representatives of local housing-advocacy or human-services organizations

The Shelter Island model, step by step

Shelter Island is the smallest of the five towns by population, and it still ran the full sequence — a reminder that the work scales down. Its fund law came first (Local Law No. 7 of 2022, adopted April 29, 2022), the voters approved it (Approved by Shelter Island voters, November 2022), the town widened the board that would write the plan (Local Law No. 1 of 2023 (January 10, 2023) grew the Community Housing Board from five members to seven, adding seats for construction, real estate, finance, environmental, housing-advocacy, and housing-recipient perspectives), and the plan followed in 2023.

The town's Community Housing Plan was drafted and taken through public workshops and a hearing during 2023, adopted on the advice of the Community Housing Board as § 64-k requires, and folded into the comprehensive plan update — the first time that plan carried a housing plan.

Sourcing gap, stated plainly: The local-law number and exact adoption date of the plan itself could not be confirmed against a retrievable primary record here. The Shelter Island Town Clerk's office holds it. Treat "adopted in 2023" as the sourced grain, not a citation. REPORTED

How Riverhead got here

2021
State law creates the optionSTATUTE

The Peconic Bay Region Community Housing Act names Riverhead as one of the five towns that may establish a community housing fund. Amended in 2022; collections in adopting towns start April 1, 2023.

April 2022
The Town Board takes it upREPORTED

Riverhead's board discusses whether to adopt the local law that would put the 0.5% tax to a townwide vote in November.

July–August 2022
Riverhead declines to put it on the ballotREPORTED

The board lets the deadline pass without adopting the local law — the only one of the five towns not to. Then-Councilman Tim Hubbard said he had first thought it could help first-time buyers, but that the proposal "doesn't really fit Riverhead" and worked better in Southampton and East Hampton. The board's stated position was that the town already carries a large share of the region's affordable housing.

November 2022
The other four towns' voters say yesREPORTED

East Hampton, Southampton, Southold, and Shelter Island approve their referendums. Riverhead residents are not asked.

2023
Shelter Island adopts its community housing planREPORTED

Drafted with its Community Housing Board, taken through public workshops and a hearing, and carried into the comprehensive plan — the statutory precondition on spending, satisfied.

September 4, 2024
Riverhead adopts its Comprehensive Plan UpdateREPORTED

Unanimously adopted, and it does carry housing recommendations — easing accessory-dwelling-unit rules among them. It is a comprehensive plan under Town Law § 272-a, not a community housing plan under § 64-k, and it comes with no dedicated revenue.

2025
Riverhead moves projects, one at a timeREPORTED

The board unanimously approves zoning changes for Northville Commons — the Family Community Life Center's affordable housing and community-center project with Georgica Green Ventures — and a separate downtown-area project wins a $1M state grant. Supervisor Hubbard, on Northville Commons: "This is something that I've supported from day one… We're losing too many young people [who are] moving off Long Island, [because they] can't afford to live here."

Today
Still no plan, and still no fundVERIFIED

Riverhead is the only Peconic Bay town without a community housing plan under § 64-k and the only one whose buyers pay 2% at closing rather than 2.5%.

What the same tax would have raised in Riverhead

This is the one part of the page built from Riverhead's own audited numbers rather than news coverage. The Town's CPF financial statements report exactly what a 2% transfer tax collected each year. A 0.5% tax is one quarter of that rate on the same conveyances, so the sizing is division, not modeling.

It is arithmetic, not a forecast

A 0.5% rate is one quarter of a 2% rate applied to the same conveyances, so the sizing is Riverhead's own audited CPF transfer-tax revenue divided by four. No model, no assumptions about the market.

The exemption floors differ, so the real figure is lower

Towns that adopted the housing fund also raised the sale-price floor exempt from the transfer tax, and widened the first-time-homebuyer exemption. Riverhead, having opted out, kept the older floors ($75,000 unimproved / $150,000 improved). Applied to Riverhead's sales, the housing-fund exemptions would shave real dollars off the quarter-share — treat the range as a ceiling.

It would have cost the preservation fund something too

Those higher exemption floors apply to the whole transfer tax, not just the new half-percent — so adopting the housing fund would also have trimmed the 2% CPF take. That trade-off belongs in the total, and this estimate does not net it out.

It assumes the sales still happen

A higher closing cost can change behavior at the margin. Nothing here models that.

Does the number pass a smell test? The estimate lands between Southold (about $4.8M by May 2025) and East Hampton (about $25.19M through 2025) — which is where Riverhead belongs: its sales volume is high and its prices are far below the South Fork's. A number above East Hampton's or below Shelter Island's would be a sign the arithmetic was wrong.

The argument, both directions

The 2022 decision had reasons behind it, and they are not frivolous. Here they are next to the case for taking the question back up — so a resident can weigh both rather than be handed one.

Reasons the Town gave, and real costs

The board's stated position: Riverhead already carries the region's affordable housingREPORTED

In 2022 board members held that the town has substantially more affordable and multifamily housing than its East End neighbors, and that a law written for the Hamptons market "doesn't really fit Riverhead."

The tax is paid by the buyerREPORTED

The Peconic Bay transfer tax is paid by the grantee at closing. A tax aimed at affordability is collected from people buying homes — which is the tension the statute's first-time-homebuyer exemption exists to answer, and a fair thing to weigh.

It raises the exemption floor on the CPF as wellREPORTED

Adopting the housing fund moves the exempt share of every sale, so some preservation-fund revenue goes with it. Riverhead's CPF is the largest revenue engine the Town controls outside the levy.

Money is not the only constraintREPORTED

Sewer and water capacity, zoning, and project-level opposition all bind. A fund does not by itself produce a unit, as Southold's slower start shows.

Reasons to take it back up

The Town's own leadership describes the problem the fund addressesREPORTED

Supervisor Hubbard, backing Northville Commons in 2025: "We're losing too many young people [who are] moving off Long Island, [because they] can't afford to live here." That is the case for a dedicated revenue source stated by the person who would administer it.

Riverhead is funding housing project by project, without a plan or a dedicated dollarREPORTED

Northville Commons, the state grant, the ADU changes in the 2024 comprehensive plan — real progress, each pursued on its own terms. The four other towns did the planning once, by local law, and attached money to it.

A plan is required before a dollar is spent — so the planning is the long poleSTATUTE

Under § 64-k the plan, the advisory board, and the smart-growth analysis all have to exist before the fund can be used. A town starting today still has that work ahead of it; the four towns that started in 2022 have it behind them.

The tax itself is not the Town Board's decision to make — it is the voters'STATUTE

The statute puts the levy to a mandatory referendum. What the board decided in 2022 was not whether Riverhead would have the tax, but whether Riverhead residents would get to vote on it.

Questions a resident can actually ask

The Town Board takes public comment at every meeting, and the vote record shows who votes how. Four questions this page supports with sourced numbers:

  1. The 2024 Comprehensive Plan Update carries housing recommendations. What dedicated revenue is attached to them?
  2. New York Town Law § 64-k still lists Riverhead. What has changed since 2022 in the board's reading of whether it fits the town?
  3. If the tax would be decided by voters at a mandatory referendum, what is the case for not letting them decide?
  4. Would the Town publish its own estimate of what a 0.5% housing tax would raise here, net of the higher exemption floors that come with it?
