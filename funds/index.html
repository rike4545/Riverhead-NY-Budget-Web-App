Funds Explorer

Every operating fund, drilled down to departments, spending categories and individual account lines: the 2026 adopted budget, reconciled to the dollar, beside what the 2027 Tentative Budget proposes.

Independent project·Updated October 18, 2026·Current·Check data freshness →

In plain English

This page shows where the Town plans to spend money, organized into separate pots called funds. The biggest is the General Fund, which pays for most town-wide services.
  • A fund: is a separate pot of money for a purpose: General (most services), Highway, Water, Sewer and so on. Each has its own balanced budget.
  • Open any fund: to see the departments and individual spending lines inside it, with what each was budgeted for 2025 and 2026 and what the 2027 Tentative proposes.
  • Tentative: the Supervisor’s proposal for 2027. The Town Board can change it before it adopts a budget by November 20.
  • Savings used: money left over from earlier years that a fund counts as income to balance its budget. A section below shows which funds use it and how much.
  • Reconciled: our line totals add up exactly to the Town’s official published numbers.

What the 2027 Tentative proposes

Leaving out the Debt Service, Workers’ Compensation and Risk Retention funds, which the other funds pay for, spending would go from $112,722,754 in 2026 to $116,864,850, up $4,142,096 (3.7%). The property tax levy across all funds would go from $65,343,939 to $67,159,863, up $1,815,924 (2.8%). It is a proposal: the Town Board can change it before it adopts a budget by November 20.

Which funds use it, and how

The all-funds total counts some dollars twice: the Debt Service, Workers’ Compensation and Risk Retention funds are paid for by transfers from the other funds, so their spending also appears in those funds’ budgets. That is why the paragraph above leaves them out. Sources: the Summary pages of the 2026 Adopted Budget and the 2027 Tentative Budget (PDF). How savings help balance the budget

The 2027 Tentative plans to use $5,895,000 of savings, down $100,000 (1.7%) from the $5,995,000 in the 2026 budget. That money is not spent on any one thing. Each fund’s budget lists its share as income, under the name “appropriated fund balance”: money left over from earlier years that pays part of this year’s bills, so less has to come from taxes and charges. Five of the 19 funds use it, and each uses only its own savings: a sewer district’s cannot pay for General Fund services.

  • The Calverton Sewer District leans on savings most. Savings would pay 42.0% of its proposed spending, about $42 of every $100. The Riverhead Sewer District’s share is 26.5%, the Water District’s 17.3%.

  • The General Fund would use $950,000, down $300,000 (24.0%), which is 1.3% of its spending. The Supervisor’s letter: “The 2027 Tentative Budget also includes a $300,000 reduction of Fund Balance contribution to the General Fund.” Its budgets planned $2,000,000 for 2024, $1,750,000 for 2025 and $1,250,000 for 2026.

Some funds put money back

The same budget sets money aside in seven funds, $3,711,453 in all ($826,751 in 2026). It does this with lines most often named : they are listed with the spending, but the money is meant to stay in the fund’s savings. Only the Budget Supplement shows them; the Summary counts them as appropriations.

A plan, not a result

The savings figure in a budget is a plan. The Budget Supplement’s 2025 actual column shows $0 on every one of these lines: savings are never received the way a fee is, and money set aside is never paid out. Whether a fund really drew on its savings shows only in its year-end results. For the General Fund, the audits show it ending each of these years with more savings, not less:

What the water and sewer funds hold

The Tentative’s own Fund Balances page (p. 4) lists the three sewer funds together at $23,794,711 and the Water District at $49,353,504, as audited at the end of 2025. Those figures are the audit’s total net position, and most of it is capital: pipes and plants, net of the debt that paid for them. What is left after capital and restricted money is unrestricted net position. For the sewer funds it was a deficit of $4,493,308, because it also counts long-term obligations such as the $6,915,305 they owe in retiree health benefits. In cash, they held $7,016,396 at the end of 2025, and $2,013,864 more that is restricted.

The sewer funds’ 2026 and 2027 budgets together plan to use $5,865,000 of savings, against $7,016,396 of cash at the end of 2025, and their net position fell by $1,092,141 in 2025. The Tentative’s page estimates each fund’s balance at the end of 2026 by subtracting a column labelled “2025 Application of Fund Balance”, $4,650,000 in all. Outside the General Fund, those are the 2025 budget’s planned uses, not 2026’s: Calverton Sewer’s is $550,000, where the 2026 budget plans $645,000. It subtracts nothing from the General Fund, whose 2026 budget plans to use $1,250,000, and leaves that fund at its audited $33,303,051.

What residents could ask

Using some savings is common. The concern comes when a fund plans to pay a large share of its spending from savings year after year: it is planning to spend more than it takes in, which works only while the savings last. Residents could ask how many more years the sewer funds can draw on savings at this pace, what would happen to sewer rents and taxes when they cannot, and whether the Tentative’s Fund Balances page should subtract the 2026 budget’s planned uses.
