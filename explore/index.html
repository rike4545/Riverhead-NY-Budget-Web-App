What “the budget” actually is

Once a year the Town Board writes down what it plans to spend and how it plans to pay for it, and votes it into law. That plan is the budget. For 2026 it comes to $121,110,904, split across 19 separate “funds” — buckets of money that can only be spent on certain things. New to any of this? The plain-language guide explains every term.

Where it comes from

About half is your property taxes

Of that $121.1M, roughly $65,343,939 is raised from the property-tax levy; the rest comes from state aid, fees, grants, and reserves. New York caps how much the levy can grow each year (~2%) — but for 2026 the Board voted to override that cap and raise the levy 7.74%. The story of that cap (and the years it was pierced by mistake) is worth knowing.

Who pays

A few big properties carry a lot of it

About 35,902 people live here, but the levy is spread across the assessed value of every taxable property — roughly $7.1B of market value. A handful of big commercial ratables (Tanger Outlets, PSEG, Costco) shoulder an outsized share, and 300+ tax grievances a year quietly shift the burden around.

Where it goes

Mostly people

The single biggest controllable cost in any town is its workforce. In 2025 Riverhead paid $37,982,278 in gross wages to 574 employees, including $1,409,969 of overtime. You can look up any employee and now see exactly how their gross pay is built — base, overtime, and the “other” extras like longevity and stipends.

The cushion

What the Town has in savings

A town keeps reserves (“fund balance”) for emergencies and to steady the tax rate. The General Fund ended 2025 with $33,407,251 in fund balance and ran a $5,003,327 surplus for the year. The Town's own policy sets a 15% floor and a 20% target against General Fund spending; the balance is currently well above both. How much of that cushion the Town leans on each year is one of the clearest signs of fiscal health.

What we owe

Borrowing pays for the big things

Roads, buildings, and water mains are paid for by borrowing, then repaid over decades — so part of every budget is last decade's decisions. Riverhead owes about $38.4M in bonds plus $22.0M in short-term notes, and carries a Aa2 credit rating. Debt service falls steadily from here, which frees room in later budgets.

What it costs you

About $6,700 a year, per household

Divide the whole $121.1M town-wide budget by the roughly 35,980 people who live here and it comes to about $3,366 per resident per year. Some of that is money moving between Town funds, and about a fifth of what is left is paid by the people who use a service — water bills, permits, beach passes. Strip both out and the part carried by taxes and general revenue is $2,533 per resident, or $6,663 per household — 7.1% of what the median Riverhead household earns. Your own share arrives as a tax bill built from your assessed value, not that average. Whether it buys a lot or a little is the fair question: Riverhead spends $22,495 per maintained mile of road, 8th of 10 among Suffolk's ten towns, against a median of $31,386.

Plan vs. reality

The budget is a promise — the audit is the receipt

The budget says what the Town intends to do; the year-end Annual Financial Report, filed with the State Comptroller, shows what actually happened. Comparing the two — where revenue came in high, where a department overspent — is where the real accountability lives.

Who decides

Every dollar is a vote

Nothing gets spent without the Town Board voting for it. We’ve logged 1,770 votes across 43 meetings — most pass unanimously, but 33 were contested. What is coming

Next year is already tight

Carried forward on contracts the Town has already signed, the 2027 levy grows about 5.5% — past what the tax cap allows by roughly $2,316,256. Add the retiree-health promise the Town has not funded, and the picture is that today's decisions are mostly next year's obligations. Debt service, at least, falls from here.
