What “the budget” actually is

Once a year the Town Board writes down what it plans to spend and how it plans to pay for it, and votes it into law. That plan is the budget. For 2026 it comes to $121,110,904, split across 19 separate “funds” — buckets of money that can only be spent on certain things. New to any of this? The plain-language guide explains every term.

The whole pie

It’s not one budget — it’s many funds

The General Fund pays for most day-to-day town services, but Highway, the Water District, street-lighting, sewer, and the ambulance district each get their own fund with its own taxes and spending. You can open any fund and drill all the way down to a single line item — reconciled to the dollar against the Town’s own summary.

Where it comes from

About half is your property taxes

Of that $121.1M, roughly $65,343,939 is raised from the property-tax levy; the rest comes from state aid, fees, grants, and reserves. New York caps how much the levy can grow each year (~2%) — but for 2026 the Board voted to override that cap and raise the levy 7.74%. The story of that cap (and the years it was pierced by mistake) is worth knowing.

Who pays

A few big properties carry a lot of it

About 35,902 people live here, but the levy is spread across the assessed value of every taxable property — roughly $7.1B of market value. A handful of big commercial ratables (Tanger Outlets, PSEG, Costco) shoulder an outsized share, and 300+ tax grievances a year quietly shift the burden around.

Where it goes

Mostly people

The single biggest controllable cost in any town is its workforce. In 2025 Riverhead paid $37,982,278 in gross wages to 574 employees, including $1,409,969 of overtime. You can look up any employee and now see exactly how their gross pay is built — base, overtime, and the “other” extras like longevity and stipends.

The cushion

What the Town has in savings

A town keeps reserves (“fund balance”) for emergencies and to steady the tax rate. The General Fund ended 2025 with $33,407,251 in fund balance and ran a $5,003,327 surplus for the year. How much of that cushion the Town leans on each year is one of the clearest signs of fiscal health.

Plan vs. reality

The budget is a promise — the audit is the receipt

The budget says what the Town intends to do; the year-end Annual Financial Report, filed with the State Comptroller, shows what actually happened. Comparing the two — where revenue came in high, where a department overspent — is where the real accountability lives.

Who decides

Every dollar is a vote

Nothing gets spent without the Town Board voting for it. We’ve logged 1,721 votes across 41 meetings — most pass unanimously, but 33 were contested.
