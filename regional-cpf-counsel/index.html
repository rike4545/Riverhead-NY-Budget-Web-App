Regional CPF counsel

A 2024 state law requires the five East End towns to share a lawyer for their Community Preservation Funds. On November 5, 2026 Riverhead's Town Board tabled the contract. Here is what the law, the contract and the Town's own resolutions say.

Independent, unofficial project·Data last refreshed November 11, 2026·Recently refreshed·See data update details →

In plain English

On November 5, 2026 the Board voted 5–0 to table resolution 2026-980, a retainer with Mary C. Wilson, Esq. as regional Community Preservation Fund counsel at $400 an hour plus expenses, split among the five towns by their shares of CPF revenue. Eleven months earlier the Board had approved the agreement among the towns that creates the office, saying a 2024 state law compelled it and had passed without a home rule message or consultation with Riverhead.
  • The fund: the Community Preservation Fund is the 2% tax on real-estate sales in the five East End towns that pays for farmland, open space and historic preservation.
  • The new office: a regional counsel gives legal opinions on how the towns run their funds, and attends a new 11-member regional advisory committee.
  • Home rule: New York’s constitution lets the State pass a law aimed at particular local governments only if they ask for it, with some exceptions. The Town’s own resolution says this law passed without one.

What the state law requires

Chapter 666 of the Laws of 2024, sponsored by Senator Anthony H. Palumbo and Assemblyman Fred W. Thiele, Jr., rewrote parts of Town Law § 64-e, the law behind the fund. It changed how the fund can pay for water-quality projects and land stewardship, and added a new subdivision 14:

“The towns in the Peconic Bay region shall jointly establish, by intermunicipal agreement, the office of regional community preservation fund counsel to render legal opinions and interpretations to facilitate the efficient and consistent administration of the funds created pursuant to this section. All such opinions and interpretations rendered shall be published on each town’s official website and shall be available to the public as a public record under article six of the public officers law.”

It also creates a regional advisory committee to advise the town boards:

“The committee shall consist of eleven (11) members. The members shall include each Town Supervisor or designee, a representative of village government to be jointly selected by the villages in the Peconic Bay region and five (5) members to be selected by the state legislative representatives representing the Peconic Bay region.”
“The office and committee required to be established pursuant to this subdivision shall be implemented no later than ninety days after the effective date of this subdivision.”

The sponsors' memo lists the fiscal implications as “None.” The law does not say how the counsel is paid; the towns' agreement does. Sources: Town Law § 64-e, New York State Senate; Senate bill S9065-A of 2024 (same as Assembly A9943-A), New York State Senate.

How it got here

  1. June 6, 2024The State Senate passes S9065-A / A9943-A, 60–1. The Assembly passes it the next day.
  2. December 21, 2024The Governor signs it as Chapter 666 of the Laws of 2024. It takes effect immediately, and adds subdivision 14 to Town Law § 64-e.
  3. January 10, 2025Mary C. Wilson, Esq. submits her proposal to serve as regional counsel, according to the retainer agreement.
  4. March 21, 2025The law’s deadline: 90 days after it took effect, the office of regional counsel and the advisory committee were to be in place.
  5. December 2, 2025The Town Board adopts resolution 2025-964, 5–0, authorizing the intermunicipal agreement “notwithstanding stated objection.”
  6. November 5, 2026The Board tables resolution 2026-980, 5–0, which would have signed the retainer with regional counsel.

Riverhead approved the agreement about eight months after the law's 90-day deadline, and the retainer it tabled would run for the 2026 calendar year, which had less than two months left.

What the contract says

From the retainer agreement attached to resolution 2026-980, in its own words:

  • Rate: $400 an hour, “together with reimbursement for additional expenses for travel time, telecopies, photocopying and printing fees, as the same may be incurred from time to time.”
  • Bills: “periodic invoices, quarterly, bi-annually or annually at the discretion of regional counsel.”
  • Who pays: each town “in proportion to each Member Town’s percentage allotment of total annual CPF revenues (last full year of realized revenue).” “No Member Town shall be responsible for payment of legal costs above their respective share as provided in the IMA.”
  • Term: “this Contract shall be effective for the 2026 calendar year.”
  • Account: “Payments shall be paid from the appropriate capital budget account.”

The resolution's Fiscal Impact Statement, dated October 23, 2026, says the cost has a fiscal impact that can be absorbed by existing resources, and names no account. The agreement among the towns it refers to, and the counsel's proposal, were not attached to either packet this site holds. Source: Town Board agenda packet, November 5, 2026 (resolution 2026-980, packet pages 39 to 43).

What it could cost Riverhead

No one has said how many hours the counsel will work, and Town Attorney Erik Howard said the Town had received no invoices. At the meeting he put Riverhead's share of regional CPF revenue at about 5.3% in 2023, against 51% for Southampton and 33% for East Hampton, as RiverheadLOCAL reported. The share is recalculated each year. At that share, Riverhead's part of a bill would be:

Riverhead's own fund took in $7,129,460 in transfer tax in 2023, and $7,033,230 in 2025, from its audited statements. The dollar risk to Riverhead is small. The Board's questions were about who controls the opinions the counsel gives. See Riverhead's CPF revenue and debt.

The home rule question

Article IX of New York's constitution limits the Legislature's power over a particular local government's “property, affairs or government.” A law that applies to some towns and not others is a special law, and the Legislature may pass one only if the local government asks for it with a home rule request, or if the Governor certifies an emergency and two-thirds of each house votes for it. Municipal Home Rule Law § 40 sets out how a town board makes the request. Source: Municipal Home Rule Law § 40, New York State Senate.

Town Law § 64-e applies only to the five Peconic Bay towns. In resolution 2025-964, the Town Board recorded that the 2024 amendment was proposed

“sometime in 2024 and without requirement for Home Rule Message, Assemblyman Thiele proposed yet another amendment to Town Law-64e”

and that

“his proposed amendments were signed into law again without consultation with or recommendation of the Town of Riverhead.”

It then resolved that

“notwithstanding stated objection to Town Law §64(e)14(a) and (b), the Town Board for the Town of Riverhead is compelled to approve an Intermunicipal Agreement”

and found that “notwithstanding the Town’s objection, the terms thereof are fair and reasonable under the circumstances articulated herein.” Members voting aye: Tim Hubbard, Kenneth Rothwell, Robert Kern, Denise Merrifield, Joann Waski. Source: Town Board agenda packet, December 2, 2025 (resolution 2025-964, packet pages 74 to 76).

The bill's record on the Senate's site lists no home rule request. Whether this law needed one is a legal question this site does not answer. Courts have upheld some laws aimed at particular places without a request, where the law serves a substantial state concern, and the fund itself exists under a state law that applies only to these five towns. What the record does show is that the Board objected in writing, approved the agreement anyway because it read the law as binding, and has not signed the counsel's retainer.

What was said on November 5

As RiverheadLOCAL reported:

“I was really offended that the state is requiring this of us,” Merrifield said. “We had no choice; we had to enter into this.”
Rothwell also supported postponing action, citing questions about the regional committee’s authority and its potential involvement in future changes to Riverhead’s CPF program.
Howard said he had not received any invoices from Wilson. He also said the other towns had signed agreements retaining her.

Residents at public comment asked:

  • why Riverhead should be required to contribute toward legal services concerning CPF matters in other towns, when Riverhead has used its comparatively limited CPF revenue mainly for farmland and open space;
  • why an attorney based in Florida was chosen, and whether travel between Florida and Long Island would be reimbursed;

Source: RiverheadLOCAL, “Post-election, Riverhead Town Board unanimously acts on all resolutions,” November 6, 2026.

Questions residents can ask

  • Will the agreement among the five towns, and the counsel's January 10, 2025 proposal, be published?
  • Which account would pay Riverhead's share, if the contract says a capital budget account and the Fiscal Impact Statement names none?
  • Are the counsel's opinions binding on Riverhead's Town Board, or advice it can decline?
  • Has any opinion been given yet? The law requires each one to be published on every town's website.
  • Did the Town ask its State legislators to amend or repeal subdivision 14, and what did they say?
