Reserves & fund balance policy

How the Town's savings stack up against its own reserve rules, what a disciplined one-time deployment plan could look like, and how Riverhead's posture compares to nearby towns.

Independent project·Updated September 25, 2026·● Current·Check data freshness →
💡In plain English
The General Fund ended FY2025 with $28,829,513 in unassigned reserves — 41.7% of the $69,113,159 2026 General Fund budget. The Board has since committed $3,896,618 of it by resolution, which puts a ceiling of $24,932,895 on what is left — 36.1%, still well above the Town's 15% policy floor. A ceiling, not a balance: there is no 2026 financial report. Every plan below is priced against that second figure rather than the opening one.
  • ›Unassigned fund balance: the "rainy-day" savings with no strings attached — the FY2025 year-end figure from the independent audit the Town Board accepted on September 1, 2026, not a mid-year estimate. It is one of five classifications; the other four are shown below.
  • ›Policy floor: Riverhead's policy, Resolution 918 of 2011, sets a 15% floor and no ceiling. It says money above the floor may be used to cut the next year's property taxes, for one-time capital costs, or for natural emergencies.
  • ›One-time vs. recurring: money above the floor is one-time money — good for debt paydown, capital or one year of tax relief, not for permanent new spending.
  • ›Opening vs. available: the audited figure for December 31, 2025. The Board has voted against it all year, so what is left is the smaller number, and it is the one every plan here is priced against.

What the Town's fund balance policy says

The policy in force is Resolution 918 of 2011, adopted 5–0 on December 20, 2011. Its floor, in its own words:

“The Town Board will make all reasonable efforts to maintain a total fund balance including reserves in its General Fund at the end of each fiscal year equal to no less than 15% of its total operating budget.”

And what money above the floor is for: “Fund balance in the General Fund above 15% may be appropriated for the following purposes:”

  • “To reduce the subsequent year’s property taxes.”
  • “For one-time capital expenditures.”
  • “For emergencies caused by natural occurrences such as hurricanes or blizzards.”

It sets no ceiling and does not require the money above the floor to be used, only permits it. It also says “fund balances and adequate reserves should be managed and reviewed on a regular basis.” It has not been revised since 2011.

If spending would take the balance below the floor, all it asks for is a vote: “If an emergency or a need were to occur that necessitated the appropriation of funds that would result in reducing the projected fund balance in the General Fund below 15% of operating expenditures, a resolution of the Town Board would be adopted to approve such appropriation.”

The 2006 policy went on: “Subsequent to such appropriation, the Town would immediately begin the process of reducing expenditures or raising revenues in order to restore the unreserved/unappropriated fund balance in the General Fund to 15% of operating expenditures.” The 2011 rewrite dropped that sentence, so the policy in force sets no way back to the floor.

What the 2011 rewrite added, for GASB 54: money is committed only by a Board resolution, passed by a simple majority before the year ends; when a budget uses fund balance, the Financial Administrator records it as assigned; restricted money is spent first and unassigned money last; and the Financial Administrator must report projected revenue shortfalls to the Board “on, at a minimum, an annual basis.”

December 5, 2006, Resolution 2006-1101: Adopted 4–0. The first policy: a 15% floor, three uses for money above it, and a promise that after any draw below 15% the Town “would immediately begin” rebuilding to it.
November 15, 2011, Resolution 2011-833: Tabled 5–0. A rewrite for GASB 54, tabled after public comment. It proposed a 10% unrestricted minimum and let the Financial Administrator assign fund balance.
December 20, 2011, Resolution 2011-918: Adopted 5–0. Amends the 2006 policy for GASB 54. It keeps the 15% floor and the three uses, adds who may set money aside and the order it is spent, and drops the promise to rebuild. The policy in force.

Correction. Until September 2026 this site described the policy as “a 15% minimum and 20% upper target.” No Town record sets a 20% target. The only 20% in either policy is the 2006 version's note that the balance at the end of 2005 was “in excess of 20%.” The figures on this site that were measured against 20% now use the 15% floor. A search of every Board agenda from 2007 through September 2026, and every set of minutes from 2012, found no later resolution changing the policy.

Sources: Resolution 918 and the policy text (Dec. 20, 2011 agenda packet, pp. 16–21); Minutes, Dec. 20, 2011 (adopted 5–0, pp. 1357–1358); Minutes, Nov. 15, 2011 (Resolution 833 tabled, pp. 1190–1191); Resolution 1101 and the 2006 policy (Dec. 5, 2006 agenda packet, pp. 72–74); Minutes, Dec. 5, 2006 (adopted 4–0, pp. 47–48).

How nearby towns write their rules

Riverhead’s 15% sits in the middle of its neighbors’ minimums, which run from Smithtown’s 5–10% to Brookhaven’s 25%. What its policy lacks, several of theirs have. Four of the six say how the balance gets rebuilt after a draw below the minimum: Brookhaven over three years, Southold within the current or next year, Huntington starting immediately and Smithtown within five years. Two direct by law what happens to a surplus: Brookhaven and Southampton. GFOA’s guidance asks for both: a rebuild within one to three years, and a policy for money above the minimum.

Each town counts different money: Riverhead and East Hampton the total balance, Brookhaven all but inventory-type items, Smithtown all unrestricted money, Southampton a set-aside reserve plus unallocated money, and Southold and Huntington only money free to spend. So the percentages are not a like-for-like ranking. Each rule comes from the town's newest audit, budget or policy document, linked on its card, and every quoted phrase is checked against that document when the site is built.

The five classifications of fund balance

Every percentage above measures one tier. GASB Statement 54 splits a fund's balance into five, ordered by how hard the money is to spend — and the Town's audit reports all five. Of $33,303,051 in total General Fund balance at the close of 2025, $28,829,513 is Unassigned — spendable on anything lawful — and $4,473,538 sits in the four constrained tiers. Every percentage on this page measures the Unassigned tier, the only money free for any use. The Town’s own policy measures the total, including reserves, so this page’s test is the stricter one.

What 2026 has already committed

The $28,829,513 above is the audited balance at December 31, 2025, from the independent audit the Board accepted on September 1, 2026. The Town's own unaudited filing with the State Comptroller had put it $841,571 higher, at $29,671,084. Through the meetings published so far the Board has committed $3,896,618 of it, which puts a ceiling of $24,932,895 on what is left: 36.1% of appropriations rather than 41.7%. A ceiling and not a balance — there is no 2026 financial report, several adopted draws state no amount so the committed side is itself a floor, and fund balance moves for reasons no resolution records. The compliance verdict does not turn on which end you take: both figures clear the 15% policy floor comfortably. What the netting changes is how much one-time money a plan can responsibly assume.

Why a ceiling rather than a balance. These are authorizations, not cash out the door — a budget adjustment permits spending, and what was consumed appears in the next financial report. Several adopted draws state no amount at all, so the committed side is itself a floor and the remainder could be lower. And fund balance moves for reasons no resolution records: revenue beating budget and departments underspending together swung the General Fund $8.6 million to the good in 2023, so it could equally be higher. The true current balance is unknown until the Town files a 2026 report. It is netted here anyway, because a dollar the Board has already voted cannot fund a suggestion on this page as well. Spending it is not the only option

Every line above uses one-time money. New York also lets a town move it into a formal reserve — a different legal thing from leaving it as unassigned fund balance. Unassigned fund balance is spendable on anything. A statutory reserve is not: the money is committed to a named purpose, and the Board has to act to get it back out. The first two below are created by board resolution and need no referendum; the tax-stabilization reserve is subject to permissive referendum. What the law does not offer is a retiree-health (OPEB) trust; there is no such reserve in the statute, which is why the Town’s $129.5M OPEB liability cannot be pre-funded no matter how large the surplus gets.

GML § 6-pEmployee Benefit Accrued Liability Reserve
Accrued leave owed at Dec. 31, 2025: $11,608,615
Pays out accumulated sick, vacation and holiday time when employees separate. The liability has risen every year since 2023, and the 2026 retirement incentive converts part of it to cash inside a single budget year.
GML § 6-rRetirement Contribution Reserve
Net pension liability at Dec. 31, 2025: $27,346,801
Absorbs swings in the Town's NYSLRS and PFRS bills. Riverhead's share moved from $21.4M to $27.3M in a single year on investment returns alone — movement the Town does not control and cannot budget away.
GML § 6-eContingency and Tax Stabilization Reserve
Ceiling for Riverhead (10% of general + highway): $7,703,241
The one written for the problem the Town actually has. It may be used to lessen or prevent a projected levy increase above 2½%, and to absorb unanticipated revenue losses. For a town the ceiling is 10% of the town-wide general and highway funds — about $77.0M for 2026. Establishing it takes a board resolution subject to permissive referendum, and spending from it needs the chief executive officer’s recommendation plus a two-thirds board vote. If the balance ever exceeds the 10% ceiling, the excess must go to reducing the next year’s levy.

NYS Comptroller, “Reserve Funds” (Local Government Management Guide); General Municipal Law Article 2. Liability figures from the 2025 Annual Financial Report, Schedule W (accounts 687 and 638).
