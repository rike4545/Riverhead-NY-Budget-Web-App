2026 Early Retirement Buyout

The Town’s 2026 Voluntary Retirement Incentive Program — the final, executed terms offered to eligible CSEA, police (PBA), and superior-officer (SOA) employees who retire this year.

Color an increase:
💡In plain English
In 2026 the Town reached agreements with all three of its unions to offer a voluntary early-retirement buyout. This page lays out exactly who qualifies, the deadlines, and what each retiree is paid.
  • ›What a buyout is: a one-time payment the Town offers to encourage eligible longtime employees to retire now, so it can reshape or trim staffing and reduce future payroll cost.
  • ›This is final language: these are the actual executed union agreements (not a proposal or a model), transcribed from the Town Board agenda.
  • ›Two deadlines: an eligible employee must commit in writing by September 1, 2026 and retire by October 1, 2026.
Status: Union stipulations executed; Town Board ratification on the July 7, 2026 agenda

The three union agreements were negotiated and executed as stipulations; the Town Board ratifies them by resolution. The recorded votes will appear in the Town Board Votes record once the July 7, 2026 minutes are published.

Who is eligible

An employee must meet all of these:

  • Employed by the Town as of June 1, 2026.
  • Voluntarily resigns from Town service for retirement purposes, effective no later than October 1, 2026.
  • Had not, as of June 1, 2026, already submitted a letter of intended separation.
  • Is not separating under any other prior stipulation, State-offered incentive, or similar program, and is not the subject of formal disciplinary action during the 2026 fiscal year.

What they must do

  • Deliver a duly executed "Irrevocable Letter of Resignation" to the Town Supervisor by September 1, 2026, stating the intended retirement date (no later than October 1, 2026).
  • Simultaneously submit a signed "Waiver and General Release of Claims."
  • The incentive payment is added to the employee's final W-2 and paid minus applicable taxes and withholdings, on top of any severance already owed under the collective bargaining agreement.
  • Retiree health insurance is unchanged: available only if the employee otherwise meets the preexisting eligibility criteria in the applicable contract.

Why this matters for the budget

Personnel is the Town’s largest controllable cost. A buyout trades a one-time payment now for the chance to hold positions vacant, consolidate roles, or refill at lower cost — turning recurring payroll pressure into a near-term expense. Used well it can support tax stabilization and reserve health; used poorly it spends money without proving lasting savings. You can see the workforce it applies to in the Payroll Explorer, and the ratifying votes in the Town Board Votes record.

What it costs — and will the Town save money?

💡The short answer
It depends on what the Town does with the vacated jobs — but the math favors savings. The payment is small next to the salaries: about $12,500 per CSEA retiree and roughly $38,734 per police retiree, against average base salaries of $60,281 (CSEA) and $116,861 (police). If jobs are refilled at a lower starting step, the Town recovers the payment in about 1–1.7 years and saves after that. If jobs are held vacant, savings are immediate. Only if every job is refilled at the same cost is it a pure one-time expense.

A more realistic backfill (using the actual salary steps)

The 20% figure above is a round number. Using the Town's actual salary schedule — where each job has a full ladder of steps from entry to top — the gap between a long-tenured retiree and a new hire is usually much larger. For the 20 eligible positions where the schedule shows an entry step, refilling each at the bottom of the scale would cut their combined salary from $1,758,423 to $1,040,359 — an annual salary saving of $718,064/yr (about 41% of their pay, not 20%).

The effect is largest for police: a top-step officer earns well over $110,000, while a new officer starts near $52,049 — so replacing one with a rookie saves roughly $60,000 a year, every year. (This is a salary figure only; an academy-trained replacement also carries training costs, and the Town may choose to hold the post open instead.)

Important: a retirement isn't always "replace with a rookie."

A vacancy can also be filled by promoting a current employee — who then gets a raise, so the savings shrink and the vacancy just shifts down to their old role. It can be filled by a transfer from another department (moving the gap elsewhere), or the position can be eliminated or restructured (which saves more). For ranked jobs like police, that's exactly what happens — a retiring sergeant triggers a promotion chain, and the rookie is hired at the bottom, so the real saving lands there, not in the sergeant's slot (modeled below). Treat the figures above as one illustrative path (direct replacement), not a guaranteed result.

Police: where the saving really lands (the promotion chain)Open ▾

Police: where the saving really lands (the promotion chain)

A ranked officer can't be replaced by a rookie of the same rank — the Town still needs a sergeant, a lieutenant, a captain. So a ranked retirement doesn't erase that salary; it sets off a chain of promotions that keeps every rank filled and ends with one rookie hired at the bottom. The recurring saving is therefore a top-step officer swapped for a rookie — the same for every ranked retirement, no matter how senior — not the retiree's own rank salary swapped for a rookie.

Retiree healthcare — the recurring cost the salary numbers missOpen ▾

What about retiree healthcare?

The savings figures above count salary only — they do not subtract retiree healthcare, which is the biggest recurring cost of a retirement. Riverhead retirees keep Town-subsidized health coverage for life. As of the 2023 audit the Town already carried a $152,597,117 retiree-health liability and paid $3,552,558 that year for 211 retirees — about $16,837 per retiree per year.

Two things cut into the salary savings: (1) the buyout pulls each retiree's ~$17k/year health cost forward, and (2) if the job is refilled, the Town then pays health for both the retiree and the new active employee — so healthcare spending for that position can nearly double even as salary falls. So the true net saving is smaller than the salary numbers: for a police officer, roughly $60k/yr in salary savings minus ~$17k/yr of added retiree health is closer to ~$43k/yr net; for a lower-paid CSEA role the health cost can offset most or all of the salary saving. (Tellingly, the 2019 incentive was retiree healthcare — 48 months of paid premiums — which is exactly why it's such a large factor.)

How does this compare to other towns?

Lifetime, Town-subsidized retiree health coverage isn't unique to Riverhead — it's standard practice across Long Island towns, all of which face the same math: a pay-as-you-go promise that grows every year retirees live longer and premiums rise. Riverhead ranks 4th-highest of these 10 Suffolk towns on a per-resident basis — behind the smaller East End towns (Shelter Island, East Hampton, Southampton, which spread a similar-sized liability across far fewer residents) and ahead of the larger western-Suffolk towns (Brookhaven, Islip, Huntington), which have much bigger populations to spread the same kind of cost across.

•Population size is the single biggest driver of the per-resident figure: East End towns are small, so even a modest total liability produces a high per-resident number; Brookhaven and Islip are Suffolk's largest towns by population, so a much bigger total liability still nets out to a small per-resident share.
•How generous each town's retiree health benefit is (lifetime full-family coverage vs. a buyback/opt-out option, contribution-sharing rules, how many years of service vest the benefit) varies by union contract and changes over time — Riverhead's own CSEA 2026-2029 contract, for example, added new retiree buyback amounts for employees who decline coverage.
•Workforce mix matters: towns with larger police forces tend to carry more of this liability, since police and fire unions have historically negotiated stronger retiree-health terms than general civilian units.

For scale, New York's own statewide NYSHIP Participating-Employer premium (the benchmark plan Riverhead's coverage tracks) runs about $1,611/month ($19,337/yr) for individual coverage and $3,664/month ($43,965/yr) for family coverage for a non-Medicare retiree, or about $596/month (~$7,157/yr) once a retiree becomes Medicare-primary. Riverhead's own blended ~$17,000/retiree/year (2023 audit) sits between those figures — consistent with a retiree population that's a mix of pre-Medicare and Medicare-eligible ages.

A note on the numbers: This table uses the Empire Center's OPEB Liabilities Tool (published Dec. 2020), which reports a 'municipal net liability' and a per-resident 'share' — a different snapshot, and possibly a different accounting scope, than the $152,597,117 OPEB figure sourced from Riverhead's 2023 audit used elsewhere on this site. Treat this as a same-source, apples-to-apples ranking across towns rather than a literal match to the 2023 figure.

How this estimate was built & its limits

  • Eligible pool is an upper bound from hire date and union; actual eligibility also requires age / retirement eligibility, so fewer people likely qualify.
  • Participation is unknown until the September 1, 2026 election deadline; scenarios show 15%, 30%, 50%, and 100% uptake.
  • These figures count SALARY only. They do not net out retiree healthcare (OPEB): each new retiree keeps Town-paid health for life (~$17k/yr), which the buyout pulls forward, and a refilled position then carries both retiree and new-active health coverage. See the retiree-healthcare section — net savings are smaller than the salary numbers shown.
  • The step-based backfill assumes each vacated role is filled by a NEW HIRE at the entry step. In practice a retirement may instead trigger a promotion (an existing employee moves up and gets a raise, which reduces the net saving), a lateral transfer (the vacancy simply moves to another role), or elimination/restructuring of the position (which increases the saving). For ranked jobs like police, a retiring sergeant is not replaced by a rookie sergeant — a senior officer is promoted and the rookie is hired at the bottom of the chain, so the real saving lands there.
  • The police sick-day payout is modeled at its 30-day maximum; many members have no excess accrual, so police cost is likely lower.
  • Accrued leave payouts owed at any separation are not counted as incentive cost (they are owed regardless of the buyout).

💡No one can be counted twice
Eligibility for 2026 is drawn only from employees still ACTIVE in the 2025 payroll (424 active employees). The 9 CSEA employees who retired during 2019 are no longer on the payroll, and a name-by-name check confirms none of them appear in the 2026 eligible list — so no one is counted in both programs.

The Town ran an earlier retirement incentive in 2019 (the '2019 Voluntary Retirement Incentive Program,' ratified as resolution 2019-538). It was structured very differently, and anyone who took it retired by December 31, 2019 — so they are not in the 2025 payroll and cannot take the 2026 program.

Important caveats

  • The total cost depends on how many eligible employees actually elect to retire, which is not known until the September 1, 2026 election deadline passes.
  • The ratification resolutions themselves state no direct fiscal impact; the incentive payouts and any replacement-hiring decisions are handled through the operating budget.
  • Retirement-benefit eligibility (ERS tier / PFRS 20-year) is set by New York State, not the Town; the incentive only adds the lump-sum payments described above.
