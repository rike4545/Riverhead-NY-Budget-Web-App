The tax cap — and Riverhead’s overrides

New York limits how fast a town’s property-tax levy can grow. Riverhead’s own auditor found the Town exceeded that cap five years in a row without ever adopting the override local law the law requires — the result of a 2018 miscalculation that went uncorrected until 2023.

💡In plain English
This page explains the cap in plain terms, documents a multi-year compliance failure the Town’s auditor flagged, and shows why it matters heading into the 2027 budget.
  • ›It caps the levy, not the rate: the “tax cap” limits the total dollars a town raises from property taxes (the levy), not the tax rate on any one home.
  • ›The ceiling: levy growth is capped at 2% or inflation, whichever is less, after a few adjustments.
  • ›To go higher: a town must pass a local law authorizing the override (60% board vote) — before it adopts the budget.

How the cap works

The limit. It caps the tax LEVY (the total dollars raised from property taxes, not the rate). OSC's formula is: prior-year levy × a tax-base-growth factor, plus prior-year PILOTs, all multiplied by an 'allowable levy growth factor' (the lesser of 2% or inflation — it has been the full 2% in recent years), minus coming-year PILOTs, plus any unused carryover — then certain costs are EXCLUDED (notably pension-contribution growth above two percentage points and some tort judgments and capital). The headline is '2%,' but the real ceiling is usually a bit higher once growth and exclusions are added.

The override. A town may exceed the limit only if it BOTH (1) passes a local law authorizing the override by a 60% vote of the governing body and (2) does so before adopting the budget. On a five-member board, 60% is three votes — so the vote is usually easy to reach; the step towns miss is adopting the local law.

Authority: Chapter 97 of the Laws of 2011, codified for towns at General Municipal Law §3-c; the State Comptroller (OSC) publishes the formula and collects a tax-cap compliance filing from every local government each year.

The finding: five years over the cap, no override law

Riverhead exceeded the cap five years running (2018–2022) without ever adopting the required override local law. The town's independent auditor traced it to an error in calculating the tax cap for the 2018 fiscal year, which then carried forward.

“The Town exceeded the 2% property tax cap for the year ended December 31, 2022 without adopting a local law in accordance with the New York State enacted Chapter 97, Laws of 2011 Real Property Tax Levy Cap and Mandate Relief Provisions.”— Town of Riverhead 2022 Audited Basic Financial Statements

The correction. The Town adopted a local law and exceeded the 2% cap, inclusive of allowable exclusions, in 2023.

Why it matters

It's a compliance violation, not a rounding issue

The tax-cap statute contemplates that a levy raised above the limit without proper authorization be placed in reserve and used to reduce the following year's levy. Five years over the cap with no override law is a genuine internal-controls failure, not a technicality.

It points to weak controls over the levy calculation

A miscalculation that went uncorrected from 2018 through 2022 means the number that sets residents' taxes wasn't being independently checked against the state formula each year.

Nine straight years over the cap (2018–2026)

Put the two halves together and Riverhead has exceeded the state tax cap every single year from 2018 through 2026 — the first five (2018–2022) without the required override law, the last four (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026) with it. Piercing the cap is not an occasional emergency here; it is simply how the Town budgets.

The Town now overrides the right way — but overriding is the new normal

Since the 2018–2022 lapse, the Board has done it correctly every time: 2023, 2024 (4.86% levy), 2025 (7.89% levy — the largest hike since the cap began in 2012), and 2026 (7.74% levy) were all proper, public overrides. The 2025 and 2026 budgets were Supervisor Tim Hubbard's (R); for 2026 his board unanimously adopted the override local law first, then passed the $121.1M budget (rate $67.031 to $71.549 per $1,000) on November 18, 2025 — just days before Hubbard narrowly lost re-election to Jerry Halpin (D) by 37 votes, so it was Halpin who took office in January 2026 to administer a budget he did not write. The worry is no longer the paperwork — it's that a 7–8% levy increase, far above the ~2% cap, has become the Town's routine, which puts real pressure on 2027 and beyond.
