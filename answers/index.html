🏠My taxes

What you pay the Town, why it changed, and what it is not.

Why did my property taxes go up this year?

The Town's total town-wide rate rose from $67.031 to $71.598 per $1,000 of assessed value for 2026 — an increase of 6.81%.

Estimate your own bill →

How much more am I actually paying?

On a home assessed at $37,200 — roughly $500,000 of market value at the Town's 7.44% residential ratio — the Town's share went from about $2,493.55 to $2,663.45. That is roughly $169.89 more for the year.

Run it on your assessed value →

Where does the Town portion of my tax money go?

Of the $71.598 rate, $61.948 is the General Fund, $8.695 the Highway Fund and $0.955 street lighting.

See the breakdown →

Is this my whole tax bill?

No — and this is the single most common misreading. Everything on this site covers the TOWN portion only. School, county, fire district and library taxes are levied by separate governments and billed separately; for most Riverhead households the school portion is the largest line on the bill.

💰What the Town spends

The size of the budget, what dominates it, and what moved.

How much does Riverhead spend in a year?

$121,110,904 adopted for 2026 across all 19 operating funds. The General Fund — the one most Town services run through — is $69,113,159 of that.

What does the Town actually spend the most on?

Police and people. Public safety is 35.8% of the 2026 General Fund ($24,761,480) and employee benefits and debt another 36.9% ($25,507,319) — together 72.7% before a single other service is funded.

🏗️Borrowing and debt

What the Town owes, what it owes it for, and what it costs to carry.

How much does Riverhead owe?

$38,423,858 in bonded debt as of December 31, 2025, plus $21,975,000 in outstanding bond anticipation notes.

The full debt schedule →

What is a BAN, and why should I care?

A bond anticipation note is short-term borrowing — the Town takes the money now and decides later whether to pay it off or convert it into long-term bonds. Riverhead is carrying $21,975,000 of them, so the decision to bond or repay is a real one with real cost either way.

What is the Town's credit rating?

Aa2 from Moody's Investors Service, affirmed February 16, 2024. Rating matters because it sets what the Town pays to borrow.

📈Is the Town in good shape?

Savings, cushion, and whether the position is improving.

Does the Town have savings?

$29,671,084 in unassigned General Fund balance — 42.9% of appropriations, against the Town's own policy of 15–20%.

Reserves and the policy →

Is holding that much savings good or bad?

Both readings are legitimate and the page gives you each. A large cushion protects the credit rating and absorbs a bad year; it is also money collected from taxpayers and not spent, sitting at more than double the top of the Town's own stated target.

🗓️What happens next year

The 2027 budget: the projection, the choices, and the deadline.

Will my taxes go up again in 2027?

If current trends carry forward with no policy change, the town-wide levy rises from $65,343,939 to $68,967,074 — 5.5%, which would pierce the state tax cap by $2,316,256. That is a projection, not the Town's budget.

How the projection is built →

What are the Town Board's options for my 2027 taxes?

There are 5: hold the levy flat, cut it, raise it inside the cap, raise it above the cap, or a hybrid of savings and reserves. Each has a levy number, a dollar amount the Board would have to find, and a different legal requirement — only going above the cap needs an override vote.

Compare all five →

Could Riverhead just freeze taxes, like Suffolk County did?

It is arithmetically possible and harder than it sounds. A flat levy has to absorb $3,514,315 of projected General Fund cost growth, and unlike Suffolk, Riverhead's police department sits inside the very fund a freeze would apply to.

What a zero-percent year takes →

When does the Board actually decide, and can I say anything?

The Supervisor files a tentative budget by September 30. The public hearing falls on or before the Thursday after the November election, and the Board must adopt by November 20. The hearing is the formal moment residents are heard.

What this site can’t answer

Just as useful as knowing what is here. These are the questions people arrive with that this site genuinely does not answer — with a pointer to who does, where one exists.

What is my actual tax bill?

This site estimates the TOWN portion from the Town's own published rate table. It does not know your parcel. For the real bill — every jurisdiction, amounts due and payment status — use the Town's official lookup.

Official tax lookup (BAS eGov) ↗

When is my payment due, and where do I pay it?

Due dates, penalty schedule and payment methods are set by the Receiver of Taxes, not by anything published here.

Receiver of Taxes ↗

Why is my school tax so high?

School districts are separate governments with their own budgets, their own boards and their own public votes. They are the largest part of most Riverhead tax bills and none of it is Town money. Nothing on this site covers them.

What is my assessed value, and can I challenge it?

Assessment is set by the Town Assessor on the annual roll. This site takes assessed value as an input — it neither sets nor holds it — and grievance is a separate statutory process with its own deadline.

Why does a particular employee earn what they earn?

The payroll here shows what was paid. It does not show why — the contract step, the assignment, the accrued time paid out. Gross pay is a fact; the explanation behind any individual figure is not something this data contains.

How to read these answers

Every figure on this page is pulled from the same data the detail pages render — none of it is written in by hand, so when the underlying budget or payroll data updates, these answers move with it. Each answer links to the page that shows the work; treat that page, not this one, as the source. Where a number is a projection rather than a reported figure, it says so.
