Zero or 1% for 2027

The 2027 Tentative raises Riverhead’s town-wide property tax levy 2.79%. This is what a 1% increase, or none at all, would take.

Independent project·Updated October 16, 2026·Current·Check data freshness →

In plain English

The 2027 Tentative raises the town-wide levy by $1,703,910, or 2.79%. Six amendments before the Town Board would cut the increase to $1,060,052, or 1.73%. A 1% increase would take $448,269 more than the amendments find. No increase would mean finding all $1,060,052 of it. Budgeting interest at the pace the Town is collecting it covers most of the first. The second leans on savings. A middle course of 1.24% to 1.58% would need no more savings than the amendments use, and keep the tax cap’s carryover. On the Supervisor’s $800,000 example home, the whole distance from the Tentative to no increase is about $118 a year in town taxes.
  • Levy: the total the Town raises from property taxes. The town-wide levy pays for the General, Highway and Street Lighting funds.
  • Rate: dollars of tax per $1,000 of assessed value. The tax cap limits the levy; a tax bill follows the rate.
  • Savings: “fund balance”: money left from earlier years. A budget can count some of it as income. It is one-time money.

Five levels for 2027

The Tentative raises each of the three town-wide funds’ levies by the same 2.79%. The Supervisor’s letter says every taxing district is within the tax cap limit of 2.79%, so a 1% or zero-percent levy would sit well inside the cap. The six amendments are proposals until the Board votes on them; the amendments page follows where they stand.

The General Fund carries $1,472,341 of the Tentative’s $1,703,910 increase, Highway $206,692 and Street Lighting $24,877. The amendments change only the General Fund and Street Lighting levies, and would take Street Lighting’s below 2026’s.

No increase would still lower the example home’s town tax a little. The levy would be spread over more assessed value: the Tentative’s levy rises 2.79% but its rate only 2.27%, a tax base about 0.50% larger. The Town sets the final rates on the final assessment roll when it adopts the budget, so the rates for the last three levels are estimates. The amendments page sets out each of the six, and the 2027 Tentative page the budget as proposed.

How the levy got here

The General Fund levy fell 2.90% in 2022, then rose 4.17%, 5.56%, 9.24% and 8.69% in 2023–2026. The 2027 Tentative would raise it 2.79%, or 1.74% with the amendments: the smallest change since 2022, when it fell.

Rates and levies can move differently because the assessment roll moves too. Both are shown because the cap governs the levy and a tax bill follows the rate.

Where the money could come from

Four sources, and one that is already spent. Each amount is what it could add beyond the six amendments, against the $448,269 a 1% increase needs and the $1,060,052 no increase needs.

1. Interest the Town is already earning Recurring, moves with rates

For years the Town budgeted almost nothing for interest on its deposits and collected far more. The General Fund budgeted $50,000 for 2026, after collecting $1,491,222 in 2025. The Tentative estimates $750,000 for 2027 and the amendments $1,000,000. By June 30, 2026 the General Fund had already collected $636,132.

In every year since 2023, each of the three funds collected at least 2.04 times its first-half interest by December. So twice the first half of 2026 is a cautious pace, and it is below what each fund collected in each of the last three years. Budgeting at it would add $366,072: 82% of what a 1% increase needs beyond the amendments, and 35% of what no increase needs.

The catch: Interest follows interest rates and the Town’s cash balances. If either falls in 2027, so will this, and the shortfall would come out of savings at the end of the year.

2. Other estimates below what the Town collects Recurring, less dependable

After the amendments, 31 more estimates in the three funds sit below what the line brought in during each of 2023, 2024 and 2025: $446,742 in all. The largest:

The catch: These are less dependable than interest. Of the 31, 17 had brought in less by June 30, 2026 than by June 30, 2025. Building inspection fees fell most: $529,984, against $832,301 a year earlier, while the amendments raise their 2027 estimate from $1,200,000 to $1,440,000. Raising estimates to past collections holds the levy down only if the money comes in.

3. Savings One-time

The General Fund’s unassigned balance was $28,829,513 at the end of 2025, 41.7% of its 2026 budget. The Town’s policy, Resolution 918 of 2011, sets a floor of 15% ($10,366,974) and no ceiling, and the first use it lists for money above the floor is “to reduce the subsequent year’s property taxes.” Resolutions adopted in 2026 have committed $4,951,618 of the $18,462,539 above the floor, and 11 more adopted draws carry no published amount, so at most $13,510,921 is left.

The Tentative counts $950,000 of savings as General Fund income, the amendments $880,000 and the 2026 budget $1,250,000. Paid from savings alone, a 1% increase would make it $1,328,269 and no increase $1,940,052.

The catch: A levy held down by savings has to be raised again, or the cost cut, the next year. The reserves page sets out what else the same balance is meant for.

4. Spending

The Board could also spend less. The Supervisor’s letter says: “In total, seventy-six merit raises (including the sixty-four above), nine promotional raises, six new full-time positions, as well as two new part-time positions, are budgeted for while remaining under the 2.79% tax cap.” It gives no cost for them, and the Supplement’s lines don’t separate them from other pay, so this page can’t price dropping any of them. The spending-reduction page lists cuts priced against the 2026 budget’s lines. They haven’t been re-priced against the Tentative, so none is counted here.

5. Already counted

The retirement incentive. The Supervisor’s letter says it “yielded a $819,000 savings directly to the General Fund for 2027.” That saving is already inside the Tentative, so it can’t pay for a lower levy as well.

One way to reach each

This is one route to each target, using the most lasting money first. It is this site’s illustration from the records above, not a proposal: the Town has proposed neither.

For 1%, the savings used would still be $287,803 less than 2026’s $1,250,000. For no increase, they would be $323,980 more. Raising the other estimates to their three-year lows could replace $446,742 of the savings, for a draw of $1,127,238, but with no cushion if any of them falls short.

The amendments also take $201,237 of equipment out of the budget ($158,300 in the General Fund, $42,937 in Street Lighting), to be bought from savings by separate votes during 2027. That is one-time money too, on top of the draws above.

Would a lower levy waste the cap’s carryover?

One argument against a smaller increase runs like this:

“because the tax cap calculation only allows a tax cap carryover into the following year of up to 1.5% of the next year’s tax cap - meaning, the Town would lose the ability to carryover a portion of the unused levy from the prior year - which is not recommended, especially with the cost of living increases are so high these days.”

It points at a real rule, but the rule does not support the conclusion.

What the rule says

A town that levies less than its limit may add the difference to the next year’s limit, up to a ceiling. The State Comptroller’s glossary defines carryover as the “Amount by which the tax levy for the prior fiscal year was below the tax levy limit for that year, but not more than 1.5% of that limit.” Its answers for local governments spell it out: “the amount of available carryover that the local government can include in calculating its tax levy limit for the coming fiscal year is the lesser of: A) The difference between the prior year tax levy limit (before exclusions) and the actual levy for the prior fiscal year Or B) 1.5 percent of the prior year tax levy limit (before exclusions).” Its instructions give an example: “While the local government did not use $120,000 of the available levy in 2012, it can only carry over up to $15,300.” And “there is no carryover permitted for unused exclusions associated with growth in pension costs or tort judgments.”

So the 1.5% is of the year in which the town levied less, not of the next year’s limit, as the argument has it.

What it would mean for 2027

The Tentative levies up to its limit. Its town-wide levy rises 2.785%: the State’s two growth factors multiplied, the 2% allowable levy growth factor and Riverhead’s 2027 tax base growth factor of 1.0077 from the Department of Taxation and Finance, which together come to 2.785%. Taking the Tentative’s levy as the limit, 1.5% of it is $943,233.

The amendments would lose nothing. Any increase of about 1.24% or more leaves no more room than can carry over. A 1% increase would leave $148,894 that couldn’t be carried, and no increase $760,677.

Why that is not a loss to residents

  • It is room to tax, not money. What would not carry over is permission to raise the 2028 levy by that much more without an override vote. No dollar leaves the Town’s accounts. The dollars not levied stay with the people who would have paid them.
  • Riverhead has had no carryover for years. Its 2025 letter: “The Town-wide 2025 Budget is 4.14% over the tax cap, due in large part to increases to insurance premiums and retirement systems contributions.” Its 2026 letter: “The 2026 town-wide budget is 4.63% over the tax cap.” The State Comptroller: “If a local government’s actual levy was equal to or more than the prior year’s calculated tax levy limit (before exclusions), there would be no amount available to be carried over.” So the 2027 limit has no carryover in it, and the Tentative, which levies to that limit, would carry none into 2028. Next to the Tentative, a 1% levy would gain $943,233 of carryover for 2028; its 2028 limit would still be lower, by the $148,894 that can’t carry and a year’s growth on it.
  • The Board keeps the power to go higher. “Local governments other than school districts may override the cap if at least 60% of their governing boards vote to approve it.” On Riverhead’s five-member Board that is three votes, and the Board overrode the cap in each of the four years before this one (Riverhead News-Review, Sept. 24, 2026: “Halpin’s Riverhead budget would end four-year streak of tax cap overrides”). A lower 2027 levy would mean a larger 2028 increase needs that recorded vote. It would not take the option away.
  • Levying now to keep room for later collects money the budget doesn’t need. If the Board adopted a 1% budget, levying the Tentative’s 2.79% anyway would raise $1,092,127 more than that budget needs, to keep $148,894 of room for 2028. And the larger levy would raise the base of every later limit too: “the total levy, including the levy for excludable expenses or the higher levy resulting from a successful override, becomes the base for the following year’s tax levy limit calculation.”
  • The cost of living cuts both ways. The allowable levy growth factor is “Either two percent or inflation, whichever is less.” High inflation does not lift the cap above 2%, and carryover is a small catch-up allowance, not a measure of what the Town needs. Taxpayers face the same prices.

The part that is fair

A lower levy does lower the base that later limits are built on, and only part of the room carries over. No increase in 2027 would leave the 2028 limit about $760,677 lower than the Tentative would, plus a year’s growth on it. If 2028 needs more than that limit allows, the Board would need the 60% vote. That is a real trade-off for the Board to weigh: taxes collected now that the budget may not need, or a recorded vote later if they are needed. Statewide, overrides are becoming more common: “For FYE 2026, 35.5% of villages and 28.6% of towns said they were planning to override the cap, up from 22.7% and 16.6%, respectively, in 2022.” The Comptroller’s advice holds either way: “I urge local officials to communicate with the public and be transparent about their fiscal challenges and plans for addressing them.”

The Town files its limit with the State Comptroller and has not published it. The limit covers more than the town-wide funds: “A county, city, town or village must enact a local law to override the tax levy limit that is based on the combined total levy for every fund and special district that falls within the municipal levy limit.” So the dollar figures in this section are an illustration on the town-wide levy.

A middle course: 1.24% to 1.58%

Between 1% and the amendments’ 1.73%, an increase of 1.24% to 1.58% would raise $737,293 to $945,299 less than the Tentative, more than the amendments’ $643,858, without the risks of going lower. This is a suggestion from this site’s arithmetic, for residents to put to the Board; the Town has not proposed it.

Two thresholds set the range. From about 1.13% up, the amendments and interest at its 2026 pace cover the gap without more savings. From about 1.24% up, the cap’s carryover is kept: at exactly 1.24%, all but $2,066 of the room carries into 2028, and from 1.25% up all of it does. 1.24% to 1.58% sits at or above both.

  • No more savings and no new cuts. Past the amendments it needs $93,435 to $301,441. Budgeting interest at its 2026 pace covers that, with $64,631 to $272,637 to spare: a cushion if interest comes in lower than that pace.
  • Carryover kept. On this page’s illustration, an increase from about 1.24% up leaves no more room under the limit than can carry into 2028; at exactly 1.24%, $2,066 would not. So it meets the carryover argument on its own terms.
  • A smaller rise for a home. On the Supervisor’s example home, it costs about $31 to $45 more than 2026, against $96 under the Tentative and $52 with the amendments.
  • What it rests on. The six amendments, including their higher estimate for building fees, which were running behind 2025 at mid-year, and interest holding near its 2026 pace. If either falls short, the difference comes out of savings at the end of the year.

The budget keeps beating itself

Against its own final amended budget, Riverhead’s 2023 General Fund took in $4,358,819 more revenue than it assumed and spent $4,004,592 less than it appropriated — of which $507,680 was encumbered rather than saved, leaving a true under-spend of $3,496,912. The budget projected a $1,863,203 reduction in fund balance. The year closed $6,741,373 to the good: a swing of $8,604,576.

The pattern held in the two years audited since. The 2024 budget planned to use $2,000,000 of savings; the General Fund’s balance rose $1,791,520 instead. The 2025 budget planned to use $1,750,000 of savings; the General Fund’s balance rose $4,899,127 instead. None of it held the levy down. It wasn’t known until each year was over, and it went to fund balance.

The fair reading: This is not proof of padding. Budgeting revenue conservatively and appropriating generously is ordinary municipal practice and the State Comptroller broadly prefers it to the reverse; an interest-rate spike in particular is not something a budget adopted the previous autumn can be expected to catch. But a pattern this large is also the reason the Town holds unassigned fund balance at 41.7% of appropriations against the 15% floor in its own policy. The surplus is not an accident of one year.

Raising estimates and budgeting a bigger draw rest on the same pattern, so they can’t both be counted from the same dollars. Revenue budgeted at the start of the year doesn’t turn up again as surplus at the end. So the real question a resident can put to the Board is narrower and more answerable than “can we afford zero percent.” It is: if the budget has been beating itself by several million a year, how much of that is now predictable enough to be budgeted for at the front end — where it would hold the levy down — rather than discovered at the back end, where it becomes surplus?

Why a town is harder to hold flat

What Suffolk pledged

“Affordability is currently at the top of every resident’s mind in Suffolk County. With that, I am announcing that I will not be raising general fund taxes for 2027. There will be no increase for Suffolk County residents.”

Suffolk County Executive Ed Romaine, third State of the County address, September 3, 2026. The County’s own headline on that release says 2026 while the text says 2027. The address was delivered in September 2026 and concerns the 2027 budget, so 2027 is the operative year.

The County credited more than $700 million in reserves, five bond upgrades since 2024, improved liquidity and conservative budgeting. A county general fund is one fund among several. Suffolk’s police district is a separate fund levied on the towns inside it, and the five East End towns, Riverhead among them, are not in it and do not pay that levy. So “no general fund increase” at the county level leaves a great deal of county spending outside the promise. Riverhead’s General Fund is not structured that way.

Riverhead’s General Fund is mostly police and benefits

Public safety and employee benefits together are 72.4% of the 2027 Tentative’s General Fund. The benefits figure includes $2,048,119 sent to the Debt Service Fund for the General Fund’s share of debt payments. Suffolk can hold its general fund flat in part because its largest public-safety cost sits in a separate district fund that Riverhead does not pay into and does not control. Riverhead’s police department sits inside the fund a freeze would apply to.

Both police contracts are open

The timing compounds it. The PBA and SOA agreements expire on December 31, 2026, and across 799 resolutions in 17 meetings, no successor for either has been put to the Board. Their only items this year are the July 7 retirement-incentive stipulations, which pay people to leave and set no wage rate. A 0% or 1% budget would be adopted while both police unions are unsettled, and a settlement would add to it.

And an unsettled contract is not a free one. Civil Service Law § 209-a(1)(e) makes it an improper practice to refuse to continue the terms of an expired agreement, so step movement carries on with no successor and no vote: $630,505 in 2027 for the Police Officer ladder alone, 39 officers advancing one rung while the 35 already at top step cost nothing. What does not continue is the across-the-board increase.

On the police side, 2027 is treading water

Seven sworn retirements are worth $679,007 a year. Step movement the Town owes with or without a contract costs $630,505 in the Police Officer ladder alone. The two nearly cancel: $48,502 to the good before a single contract is negotiated, and before the detective, sergeant and superior-officer ladders, which this floor does not include, are counted at all.

These are not strictly additive. Some of the retiring officers were at top step and so were not going to move anyway, which is already reflected in both figures being computed from the same authorized-salary listings. The point is the order of magnitude, not a net line in a budget.

So, could Riverhead do it?

1.24% to 1.58%

Yes. 1.24% is about the lowest increase that keeps the cap’s carryover and needs no more savings than the amendments already use: the amendments, plus interest budgeted at its 2026 pace, with $64,631 to $272,637 of that interest to spare.

A 1% increase

Yes, without cutting a service. The six amendments bring the increase to 1.73%. Budgeting interest at its 2026 pace, still below what each fund collected in every year since 2023, adds $366,072, and the last $82,197 could come from savings: a General Fund draw of $962,197, still less than 2026’s $1,250,000. Most of it would be recurring revenue, though interest falls when rates or balances do.

No increase

For one year, yes, but it leans on savings. After the amendments and interest, $693,980 would still be needed. From savings, that is a General Fund draw of $1,573,980, $323,980 more than 2026’s. The Town’s policy allows it, and what is left above its floor, at most $13,510,921, is more than 19 times that. Raising the other estimates to their three-year lows could cut the draw to $1,127,238, but leaves no cushion if any of them falls short.

The point to take away: On the Supervisor’s $800,000 example home, the Tentative’s town-wide tax is about $118 a year more than no increase would be, and the amendments’ about $31 more than 1%. Small for one household, the gap is $1,703,910 for the Town. A 1% budget built on revenue the Town actually collects could last. A zero-percent budget paid partly from savings is a one-year choice: whatever comes from savings has to be found again in 2028. GFOA’s recommended practice is to pay recurring costs from recurring revenue, so a draw like that should be adopted as a deliberate choice, not presented as costless.

How this page was made

  • The 2026 and Tentative levies and rates are the Summaries’ “Total Town Wide” rows. The amended levy applies the six resolutions’ own lines to the 2027 Budget Supplement, which adds up to the Tentative to the dollar.
  • A 1% increase is $61,790,075, 1% over 2026’s $61,178,292, rounded to the dollar. No increase is 2026’s levy.
  • Rates for the last three levels scale each fund’s Tentative rate by its levy, on the Tentative’s assessed values. For 1% and no increase, each fund’s levy rises by the same share, as each does in the Tentative.
  • The example home is the $800,000 property in the Supervisor’s letter, at the 2025–26 residential assessment ratio of 7.44% on the Receiver of Taxes’ rate sheet. Only town-wide taxes are counted: school, county, fire, library and special district taxes are not. The Department of Taxation and Finance’s municipal profile for Riverhead lists a newer ratio, 7.03% for 2026; at that ratio the home would be assessed at about $56,240, and each tax figure here would be about 6% smaller. This site uses the rate sheet’s ratio on every page, so the pages agree.
  • Interest at its 2026 pace is twice what each fund had collected by June 30, 2026, the Supplement’s year-to-date column.
  • Other estimates count lines the Town still budgets, other than property taxes, savings, interest, and charges or transfers between Town funds.
  • Collections are the Town’s books before the audit, as each Supplement prints them. Savings are the audited balance less the 2026 draws on the record.

Every figure on this page is checked against those records when the site is built (scripts/verify-zero-percent-2027.mjs). This page is unofficial. It describes likely effects for residents; it is not a forecast, an audit or legal advice.
