The downtown Pattern Book

What the 2021 design guide asks for, why it was never written into the zoning code, and why doing that now may be a hard sell.

Independent, unofficial project·Data last refreshed November 13, 2026·Recently refreshed·See data update details →

In plain English

In January 2021 the Town Board adopted the Pattern Book, a guide written by Urban Design Associates after public surveys and forums. Its main ask is shorter buildings on Main Street: four stories instead of five. The Board adopted it as advice, not law. Every attempt since to make it law has stalled, even though the Town’s 2024 Comprehensive Plan recommends it. The book’s standards and the votes are quoted from the Town’s records; what people said in debate is from news reports, labelled as such.
  • Pattern Book: a design guide for new buildings: how tall, how much of the lot, what they look like from the street.
  • DC-1: the Main Street zoning district downtown, where the book’s standards would apply.
  • Codify: write into the zoning code, so a rule binds rather than advises.

What the book asks for

Rule on Main StreetZoning todayPattern Book
HeightFive stories (60 feet)Four stories or 50 feet, stepped back above the third
Lot coverage80%, or up to 100% by applicationUp to 100%
Green roofNone requiredAt least 40% of the lot
EnergyNone requiredZero net energy strongly encouraged

The book’s trade is height for coverage: “This document proposes four stories but with 100% lot coverage with a slight stepback on the fourth story.” (page 62) It says this “this maintains approximately the same permitted developable square footage for any lot.” (page 62) Its standards table sets a height of “4 stories or 50' maximum” (page 63) and a green roof of “40% minimum of the lot area” (page 63)

From the Downtown Riverhead Pattern Book (Urban Design Associates, January 2021), pages 24, 62 and 63.

Five years as advice

  1. January 20, 2021. The Town Board adopts the Pattern Book as a planning guide, after a study that cost about $175,000. It is not part of the zoning code. (RiverheadLOCAL, March 16, 2023)
  2. May 2022. A hearing on a law to write the book into the Main Street (DC-1) zoning: four stories or 50 feet instead of five stories or 60 feet, a 40% green roof and required design elements. Residents, the Chamber of Commerce and the Downtown Revitalization Committee spoke for it; an architect on the Town’s parking committee called the design rules “subjective”. The law was put on hold after the Business Advisory Committee objected. (RiverheadLOCAL, May 5, 2022; RiverheadLOCAL, May 13, 2022)
  3. March 2023. The Business Advisory Committee brings a marked-up version to the Board. Officials decline to move away from the book and send the committee back to work with the Downtown Revitalization Committee and staff. No revised law followed. (RiverheadLOCAL, March 16, 2023)
  4. July 2024. Discussing the draft Comprehensive Plan, Board members say they want to keep five-story buildings downtown and to treat the book as a “guiding document” rather than code. A Town planner says that without codification it is “almost useless”. (RiverheadLOCAL, July 2, 2024)
  5. September 4, 2024. The Board unanimously adopts the Comprehensive Plan Update (Resolution 2024-760), which says “It is recommended that the Pattern Book be formally included in the zoning code, so it is required to be considered during the site plan approval process by developers, land use boards, the Town Board, and Town staff.” This site has found no Board action on that since. (Resolution 2024-760; 2024 Comprehensive Plan Update, page 196)
  6. March 17, 2026. Supervisor Halpin abstains on Resolution 2026-257, preliminary site plan approval for the Zenith building addition at 301 East Main Street, which passes 4–0. In the meeting’s machine transcript he says the Pattern Book asked the Town not to go to a fifth floor. (Minutes of March 17, 2026; machine transcript (may mishear words))
  7. August 4, 2026. The Board approves the five-story Peconic River Hotel at 117–127 East Main Street, 5–0 (Resolution 2026-746). The Town’s environmental review had required projects to follow the Pattern Book, and the developer’s analysis said the hotel does, with its upper stories set back from Main Street. (Resolution 2026-746; Resolution 2025-263 (March 18, 2025 packet); June 2026 agenda packet)

The 2024 plan’s recommendation is tracked on the Comprehensive Plan tracker, and the hotel on the Town Square page.

Why writing it into law may not be politically astute

This is this site’s reading of the record, not a finding and not advice to any official. Whether the book should become law is for residents and the Board. Here are the political risks the record points to:

  1. The votes are not there. In July 2024 Board members said they wanted to keep five stories and treat the book as a guide (RiverheadLOCAL, July 2, 2024). Council Members Rothwell, Kern and Merrifield, who spoke that way then, were still on the Board in 2026. A law that is expected to fail costs its sponsor without changing anything.
  2. It would put the Town’s own projects out of step. The Board approved the five-story Peconic River Hotel 5–0 in August 2026 as the anchor of the Town Square project. A four-story cap written into law now would invite the charge that the Town held private builders to a rule its own flagship did not meet. Opponents of the Town Square project, which has already brought eminent-domain fights, could cite it.
  3. Existing buildings would stop conforming. Council Member Merrifield said codifying it would put existing downtown buildings out of compliance (RiverheadLOCAL, July 2, 2024). The book itself says the buildings put up in the five years before it were “between four and five stories, a height required for developers to recoup their investment on the cost of construction and land values.” (page 5) An owner of a nonconforming building can find rebuilding or refinancing harder.
  4. It cuts against the State’s housing push, and the money tied to it. Riverhead is a certified Pro-Housing Community. That status won the Town just over $3.5 million from the State’s Pro-Housing Supply Fund for The Vue, a five-story, 133-unit building on West Main Street (RiverheadLOCAL, December 30, 2025). Lowering the height limit downtown reduces how many homes it can hold. This site has found no rule that a height cut would cost the certification, but it would sit awkwardly in the next application, and the Supervisor has himself spoken of the strain of being asked to be a pro-housing community (RiverheadLOCAL, October 6, 2026).
  5. The required parts carry costs, and organized opponents. The 40% green-roof minimum and the design rules drew the 2022 objections that stopped the law; two Council Members then preferred incentives to a green-roof mandate (RiverheadLOCAL, May 5, 2022). The Business Advisory Committee has opposed the code twice. Builders, architects and landowners are a small group, but an engaged one.
  6. The fifth story has a use the book does not count. Council Member Kern argued that a builder who wants a fifth story should be able to buy development rights to get one (RiverheadLOCAL, July 2, 2024). Buying development rights is how the Town’s transfer program pays to preserve farmland and open space elsewhere, so a hard cap can be framed as a loss for farmland preservation as well as for builders.

The case for it, and why it will not go away

  • Residents asked for it. The 2020 survey behind the book favored limiting downtown to four stories (Riverhead News-Review, October 2020), and residents spoke for the law in 2022 (RiverheadLOCAL, May 5, 2022).
  • The Town paid about $175,000 for the study, and its own planner said that without codification it is “almost useless” (RiverheadLOCAL, July 2, 2024).
  • The Board’s own Comprehensive Plan, adopted unanimously in 2024, recommends writing it into the code. Leaving that undone is an opening for any challenger.
  • The book claims its trade keeps about the same floor area per lot, which, if right, answers much of the cost objection.

A middle path is in the record too. The 2024 plan asks only that the book be “required to be considered” at site plan review, not that every standard bind. A law that requires review against the book, keeps five stories with a set-back fifth floor (which Board members said they would allow, per RiverheadLOCAL, July 2, 2024) and turns the green roof into an incentive would carry out the plan while avoiding most of the fights above.
