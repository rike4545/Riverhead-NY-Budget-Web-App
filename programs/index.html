What the Town Does, and What It Costs

Every budget from 2023 to the 2027 proposed budget, regrouped into services rather than funds — full cost including pension and health insurance, fees earned back, and what each one leaves for the tax levy. Pick a year and compare it with any other. In the 2026 adopted budget: $2,490 per resident, all in.

Independent project·Updated October 18, 2026·Current·Check data freshness →

In plain English

A budget organized by fund answers an accountant's question. This page reorganizes the same dollars by what the Town does with them — and the classification isn't ours. New York's Uniform System of Accounts already assigns every municipal dollar to a function, and Riverhead codes to it on both the spending and the revenue side. We regrouped; we didn't reclassify.
  • Full cost, not just the department line: pension and health insurance are pushed back onto the programs whose staff earned them, so Police costs what Police actually costs.
  • "Earns back": is fees paid by the people who use the service — permits, water bills, beach passes — not taxes.
  • Net is what general revenue covers: property and sales taxes, mortgage tax, PILOTs and state aid. A program with a high cost and high recovery may ask less of you than a cheaper one that charges nobody.
  • Every year, the same way: each budget from 2023 on is regrouped by the same rules, so a change from one year to the next is a change in the budget, not in the method.

Riverhead runs its own police department, and that single choice dominates the Town budget. Once pension and health insurance for sworn officers are counted, Public Safety is the largest thing the Town does by a wide margin: 36% of everything in the 2026 adopted budget. What this page still cannot tell you

GFOA’s criteria for a program budget also ask “What are the goals?” For a town, that would mean targets such as response times, permits issued, tons collected or program participation. The Town publishes no performance measures, so there is nothing to report. Cost and revenue are answered here; service-level goals are not, and no amount of rearranging the budget will produce them.

How this was built

Rearranging a budget is exactly where a transparency site can mislead without meaning to, so every decision that moved a dollar is written down here.

  1. The programs are the State’s classification, not ours
    New York’s Uniform System of Accounts assigns every municipal dollar to a function, and Riverhead codes to it. Department 3120 is Police because the State says 3120 is Police. The seven headings below are that system, not a grouping this site invented.
  2. Revenue is matched to programs by the Town’s own account tag
    The last segment of every revenue account carries the same function group as the spending side. Building Inspection Fees end in 3, so they land against Public Safety; Site Plan Fees end in 8 and land against Home & Community Services. Revenue with a letter instead — property tax, sales tax, PILOTs, mortgage tax — belongs to no single program and is reported separately.
  3. Pension and health insurance are pushed back onto the programs that earned them
    $27.4M of benefits sits in function 9 in the 2026 adopted budget, attached to no service. Accounts ending -UNI- are sworn police and go entirely to Public Safety; -NON- accounts are spread across programs in the same fund by their share of Personal Services. The uniformed/non-uniformed split is the Town’s own coding.
  4. Utility overhead follows the utility
    Insurance and depreciation booked to function 1 inside a single-purpose fund are the cost of running that utility, not general government, so they follow the fund’s service. This moves $5.4M in the 2026 adopted budget, mostly sewer and water depreciation. The General Fund and the two internal-service funds are left alone.
  5. Interfund transfers are excluded
    $8.5M of function 9901 in the 2026 adopted budget is one Town fund paying another. Counting it as program spending would double-count real dollars, so it sits outside the program totals.
  6. Money set aside is reported on its own
    Account 9990, “Fund Balance Contribution”, is money a fund budgets into its savings instead of spending it: $826,751 in the 2026 adopted budget. It pays for no service that year, so it sits outside the program totals. This page used to call it contingency.
  7. Every year comes from the same kind of record
    The Town prints each adopted budget again, line by line, in the next year’s Budget Supplement, and its Tentative Budget in that year’s own. A year is shown only when those lines add up to the budget’s Summary page for every fund, on both the spending and the revenue side: 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2027 proposed. 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022 are left out because their lines do not add up. A Tentative Budget is the Supervisor’s proposal, not a budget the Board has adopted.
  8. Staff counts are the one thing we classified ourselves
    Payroll department names are operational units — "Squad 4 - Police" — rather than account codes, so mapping them to functions is our work, not the Town’s. Each budget year uses that year’s payroll, or the latest one (2025) for years the payroll has not reached; all 574 people in the 2025 payroll are accounted for. Headcount is bodies on the payroll, not full-time equivalents, so seasonal recreation staff count the same as a full-time clerk.
  9. Per-resident and per-household are scale, not a bill
    Population is the Census Bureau’s 36,394 estimate; households are 13,678 ± 571 from American Community Survey 2023 5-year estimates (2019–2023). Dividing the Town’s net cost by either one says how big Town government is relative to the people in it, and the same counts divide every year. It is not what you owe — commercial property carries a large share of the levy, and your actual bill depends on your assessment.
